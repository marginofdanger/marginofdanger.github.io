1. Business description, model & unit economics

Arm designs and licenses the instruction set architecture and the CPU implementations in roughly 350 billion shipped chips (20-F FY2026, Item 4.B). It does not manufacture, and until March 2026 it had no hardware product of its own. Customers are the companies that design chips — semiconductor vendors such as Qualcomm, MediaTek, Broadcom, NXP and Renesas; device makers designing in-house such as Apple and Samsung; and cloud providers designing in-house such as Amazon, Google, Microsoft and Meta (20-F FY2026, Item 4.B). They pay twice: once for access to the design, and again on every chip that ships.

Every licence structure — Arm Total Access (annual portfolio fee including the newest cores, no tape-out fee), Arm Flexible Access (low annual fee on the older portfolio plus a per-tape-out fee), a Technology Licensing Agreement (fixed fee, one design) and an Architecture Licence Agreement (fixed fee, right to build a custom Arm-ISA CPU) — obliges the licensee to pay a per-chip royalty afterwards (20-F FY2026, Item 5.A). A design win at an existing customer does not require a new licence.

Revenue decomposes to volume × rate, and each term reduces to something independently measurable.

A1 — smartphone applications processors. Arm's applications-processor share has been above 99% "for many years" and cannot rise (20-F FY2026, Item 4.B), so the exposure is the literal count of handsets sold, multiplied by the applications processor's own ASP where contracts are priced as a percentage of it. The exposure is not handset dollars: the memory-driven $100 rise in handset ASPs accrues to memory makers, not to the applications processor. Contribution: 43% of FY2026 royalty ($1,124m), and $493m of the $811m two-year royalty increase, or 61%. Robustness: cyclical and currently negative — CY2025 shipments were 1.26bn, +1.9%; IDC forecasts a 13.9% decline to 1.09bn units in CY2026 on memory-led bill-of-materials inflation (IDC, accessed 2026-08-03) [web], and Gartner models −8.4% (Gartner, accessed 2026-08-03) [web]. Realized check: in FY2025 the smartphone line contributed the entire royalty increase. Mobile applications-processor royalty went from 35% of $1,802m to 46% of $2,168m, an increase of $366m against a total royalty increase of $366m. Non-mobile royalty was $1,171m in both years, and stress-testing the "approximately" in the 20-F bounds the non-mobile change between −$20m and +$20m. That composition reversed in FY2026: non-mobile royalty grew about 27% while mobile grew 13%.

A2 — IoT, embedded and consumer. Microcontroller and consumer-SoC unit shipments on mature nodes: roughly half of the 31bn unit count and 30% of FY2025 royalty at $0.02–$0.03 a chip. Contribution to growth is close to zero, and the reason is disclosed on Arm's own slide: the market value of these two markets shrank between FYE22 and FYE26, consumer electronics $72bn to $67bn and IoT/embedded flat at about $50bn [deck: Q1 FYE27 investor presentation 2026-07-29, slide 31]. These markets supply Arm's unit count. That is also why Arm's total unit count went from 30.6bn (FY2023) to about 31bn (FY2025) while royalty grew 29%.

A3 — automotive. Light-vehicle production multiplied by Arm-based compute content per vehicle, measurable from vehicle build rates and from the ADAS SoC shipments of NVIDIA, Qualcomm, Renesas, NXP and Texas Instruments. Contribution: 7% of FY2025 royalty ($152m) and 8% of FY2026 ($209m). Over FYE22–FYE25 the automotive chip market grew from $15bn to $22bn and Arm's share from 36% to 44%, and both the market and Arm's share in it rose materially. Realized: confirmed.

A4 — data centre. The number of CPU sockets hyperscalers deploy, multiplied by Arm's share of them. Cumulative Neoverse cores passed 1.5 billion, with the most recent 500 million shipping in nine months against six years for the first billion (Q1 FY27 call, CEO). Arm states its share of CPU compute among top hyperscalers is "about 50%" (Q4 FYE26 shareholder letter) and that Arm technology is in close to 100% of DPUs and SmartNICs (Q4 FY26 call, CFO). Contribution: management says data-centre royalty "more than doubled year-over-year" in each of the last three quarters, and calls Cloud AI the largest driver of royalty growth. Realized: confirmed and accelerating, but the level is not disclosed, and see §6 for what the geographic cut says about it.

B1 — architecture mix. Directly observable while Arm disclosed it: Armv9 was about 10% of royalty in the September-2023 quarter, 15% in December 2023, 20% in March 2024 and 25% by the September-2024 quarter, then stalled at 25% for three quarters before the series was withdrawn (quarterly earnings calls; Q4 FYE24 shareholder letter). Arm has stated a 60–70% target for the same metric "in the 2- to 3-year time frame" (Q4 FYE24 call, reaffirmed Q3 FYE25) and then stopped publishing the measuring metric one quarter later, describing it as "a bit noisy" (Q4 FYE25 call, CFO). This is the driver that showed up: realized royalty per chip rose about 11.9% a year over FY2023–FY2025 against a mix shifting 8–9 points a year toward a higher-rate tier, and no other driver is large enough to explain the gain.

B2 — CSS. Compute Subsystems are priced at roughly double the Armv9 royalty rate ("the royalty rate on CSS is roughly double that of v9", Q3 FY2025 call). Cumulative CSS licences signed: 16 by July 2025, 21 by February 2026, plus two in each of the last two quarters, so roughly 25 as of July 2026 [estimate: additive from disclosed quarterly signings; the cumulative counter was withdrawn from the deck after Q3 FYE26]. The deck puts CSS at about 15% of total royalty by early 2026 [deck: Arm Everywhere keynote 2026-03-24, slide 18] and the CEO said "almost 20%" on the same day, against roughly 25% of mobile royalty [deck: Arm Everywhere investor session 2026-03-24, slide 8]. The two figures are not reconciled. Partially demonstrated: the licence count and the price ladder are disclosed, the realized contribution is not, so B2 cannot be separated from B1 in the numbers.

B3 — cores per chip. Data-centre core counts went from about 8 in a high-end cloud chip in FYE16 to 192 in 2025, and Arm's scatter of customer designs runs toward 250–300 by 2028–2031 [deck: Q1 FYE27 2026-07-29, slide 20]. Royalty scales with core count. This is the mechanism by which a 14%-of-royalty end market grows 68% in a year on a socket count that is not growing 68%.

B4 — licensee chip ASP. Arm does not disclose the split between percentage-of-ASP and fixed-fee-per-chip contracts, so this cannot be isolated in any realized period. It is labelled a hypothesis. It matters in FY2027 specifically: handset ASPs are rising on memory inflation, but the memory is not inside the applications processor, so Arm may get the unit decline without the price offset. Arm's own risk factor runs the other way over a product generation — the industry is "generally characterized by declining average selling prices over the life of a generation of chips" (20-F FY2026, Item 3.D).

B5 — contractual step-downs. "The royalty rates per chip typically reduce over time as the total volume of chips incorporating our products shipped increases", subject to "a minimum royalty percentage or fee per chip" (20-F FY2026, Royalty Revenue). This effect is permanently negative and Arm has never sized it. This is the mechanical explanation for the flat decade in royalty per chip: on a processor-only basis the legacy entity earned $0.048 (CY2013), $0.045 (CY2014) and $0.048 (CY2015) per processor shipped [estimate: US-dollar total royalties scaled by the disclosed processor share of royalty, divided by disclosed unit shipments; legacy 20-F FY2015, Item 5], against $0.055 in FY2023. Mix improvements were being handed back through volume step-downs on mature designs for a decade. Any forward model that applies a rising rate to the whole installed base without a step-down drag will overstate.

Arm's investor-deck framing is "More Chips + More Share Gains + More Arm Technology" [deck: Q1 FYE27 2026-07-29, slide 12], with "More Chips" supported by a claim that semiconductor industry revenue has compounded at about 10% for ten years. On Arm's own numbers the market-growth pillar is the weakest of the three: Arm's target-market chip value grew 2.2% a year over that window, not 10%, and end-market growth accounts for a fifth of the royalty increase against 39% for share and 41% for the take rate.

The most recent year cannot be decomposed the same way, because Arm retired the unit count. FY2023 royalty was $1,675m on 30.583bn chips, or $0.055 per chip (424B4 2023, KPI table). Cumulative shipments were 287.4bn at 2024-03-31 and "more than 350 billion" at 2026-03-31, and the cumulative figure is rounded down to the nearest ten billion — the FY2025 20-F similarly says "more than 310 billion" — so the two-year run-rate is bounded at 31.3bn to 36.3bn a year and FY2026 royalty per chip at $0.072 to $0.084 [estimate: $2,613m divided by that bounded unit range]. Across the whole range, royalty per chip rose 31% to 53% in three years while units grew roughly 1% to 6% a year. That direction is not sensitive to the assumption; the level is.

The licence line has fewer drivers, and one branch has no market driver at all. Arm's-length licence revenue reduces to worldwide leading-edge chip-design starts and the engineering budgets that fund them at roughly fifty to sixty named organisations — a claim on customers' design spend, not on chip volumes, and independently measurable through EDA and design-IP vendor revenue and advanced-node tape-out counts. Arm China's share reduces to PRC design activity filtered through a counterparty Arm neither controls nor observes, at an undisclosed revenue-share percentage, recognised net. The SoftBank Consulting Agreement reduces to SoftBank Group's own capital allocation: no end market, no unit and no price-times-volume relationship.

1.4 The Arm AGI CPU

The chip had zero revenue in FY2026 and zero in Q1 FY2027. It was announced 2026-03-24, delivered to customers for evaluation during Q1 FY2027, and first revenue is guided to Q4 FY2027 at roughly $90m, with roughly $910m in FY2028 (Q4 FY26 call, CFO).

Arm has published the physical specification: up to 136 Neoverse V3 cores at 3.5 GHz, 2 MB of dedicated L2 per core plus 128 MB of system-level cache, 300 W TDP, TSMC 3 nm, dual chiplet with memory and I/O on each die, twelve channels of DDR5-8800 giving 6 GB/s sustained per core and sub-100 ns latency, 96 lanes of PCIe Gen6 with CXL 3.0 (arm.com, accessed 2026-08-03) [web]; Arm Everywhere keynote, 2026-03-24). A 36 kW air-cooled OCP rack holds 60 sockets and 8,160 cores; a 200 kW liquid rack holds 336 sockets and more than 45,000 cores. Arm does not disclose the price.

Arm's own disclosure that its FYE26 Cloud AI IP/CSS opportunity is $2.4bn against $24bn of whole-chip value on the same silicon, divided by a Neoverse core run-rate of roughly 500–600m cores a year, gives $40–$48 per core and $5,440–$6,530 per 136-core socket. Merchant comparables priced per core give $28.9 (Ampere AmpereOne A192-32X at $5,555 list), $85.4 (AMD EPYC 9755) and $97–$139 (Intel Xeon 6980P), a $3,930–$11,600 range for a 136-core part [web, ServeTheHome / WCCFTech / Yahoo Finance, accessed 2026-08-03]. The two overlap at $5,000–$6,500. Adopted here: $5,500 per socket, range $4,000–$7,000 [estimate: Arm's own TAM-to-royalty ratio divided by the Neoverse core run-rate, cross-checked against merchant per-core comparables].

A die-area build from the published specification puts silicon at $200–$300 a socket on TSMC N3 wafers at $19,500 (siliconanalysts.com, accessed 2026-08-03) [web], test at $40–$80 and substrate and assembly at $110–$200, for a landed manufacturing cost of $350–$580. Against a $5,500 ASP that is roughly 90% gross margin on the silicon Arm buys. Management guides the first generation to "high 30% range, maybe low 40s" (Q4 FY26 call; reaffirmed Q1 FY27), which implies cost of goods of $3,190–$3,410 a socket. The unexplained gap is $2,610–$3,060 per socket, 47% to 56% of revenue.

Management has not separated the explanations. The CFO attributes the path from 40% to 50% entirely to insourcing work "that maybe an [ASIC partner] has helped us with in the past", which "will probably take a couple of years" (Q1 FY27 call) — a 10-point uplift on a $5,500 ASP is $550 a socket recovered, and only "some" of the work comes in-house, so the partner's take is plausibly $1,100–$1,650 [estimate]. That closes 40–60% of the gap. Second, first-generation programme cost amortised through cost of sales: every $100m over a 182,000-unit first generation is $550 a socket, or 10 points of gross margin, and it decays automatically with volume. Third, third-party product content recognised gross. The FY2026 20-F introduced a policy for the first time — "Revenue is recognized on a gross basis when the Company controls the product or service, and on a net margin basis when it acts as an agent" (Note 1) — and disclosed approximately $100m of twelve-month purchase commitments to arrange for "certain semiconductor products to be supplied by a third party to a customer", expected to "grow materially in subsequent years" (Item 3.D). The CEO separately named memory as one of four inputs Arm must secure. Bundling 384 GB of DDR5 per socket at 2026 prices blends a 90%-margin chip to 47.9%, and 768 GB blends it to 33.7% [estimate: $31.75/GB median, rampricesusa, accessed 2026-08-03 [web]], and the guided 38–42% band sits between those two. The arithmetic fits, but the policy language is standard ASC 606 principal-versus-agent wording, it never mentions the AGI CPU, and the same paragraph provides for net treatment. Whether the first quarter of chip revenue is presented gross or net will settle it.

Per $1,000 of chip ASP: CPU IP earns roughly $50 of gross profit at a ~5% royalty rate; a Neoverse CSS earns roughly $100 at ~10%; selling the whole chip earns roughly $500 at ~50% gross margin [deck: Arm Everywhere investor session 2026-03-24, slide 40]. That is a 5x to 10x gain in gross profit dollars on the same silicon. The cost is a lower gross margin: at management's FY2031 mix of $10bn of IP at roughly 98% and $15bn of chip at roughly 50%, blended gross margin is 69.2% against 97.5% today [estimate: management's disclosed revenue split combined with its guided chip gross margin; the FY2031 slide discloses operating margins, not gross margins].

There is no inventory line at 2026-03-31 or 2026-06-30 for a product entering production by end-calendar-2026, and no wafer, packaging or test purchase commitment in the FY2026 20-F — its $1,057m of non-cancelable purchase obligations are for "cloud computing web services, data centers, software, license and services". The only product-supply commitment disclosed is the ~$100m arrangement above. Against that, the CEO says "we have secured the manufacturing capacity needed to support the $1 billion opportunity" (Q1 FY27 call). Either the capacity is held by the turnkey partner, or the commitments were entered after the balance-sheet date, or "secured" describes an allocation understanding short of a binding order.

At $5,500 a socket the supply-secured $1bn is 182,000 sockets, 3,030 air-cooled racks, about 109 MW of rack power — and Meta's Prometheus cluster alone exceeds 1 GW by end-2026 (Santosh Janardhan, Meta, Arm Everywhere, 2026-03-24). The entire two-year supply-secured programme is roughly a tenth of one Meta cluster. The whole $1bn also needs roughly 1,700–2,500 TSMC N3 wafers [estimate: 182,000 sockets ÷ 73–106 sockets per wafer] against N3 capacity of 160,000–180,000 wafers a month [web, TrendForce / WCCFTech, accessed 2026-08-03] — about 1.0–1.5% of one month's output. The constraint is DDR5 allocation, ABF substrate, automated test slots and Arm's queue position as a first-time buyer, which is what the CEO described: "You have wafers, you have substrates, you have test capacity, you have memory" (Q1 FY27 call).

2. Key technologies & technical differentiators

Arm has one hard differentiator and one real engineering competence, and neither is the CPU microarchitecture.

The instruction set architecture is the hard part, because of the software attached to it. The ISA is "a common language for software developers. It sets the foundation for a large library of compatible software which runs on those CPUs" (20-F FY2026, Item 4.B). Arm owns the specification, the trademark and the compliance regime, and charges a royalty on substantially every chip that implements it whether or not Arm designed the CPU inside. The barrier is not the document. Arm's IPO prospectus quantified the accumulated investment: Arm engineers spent "more than 10 million hours in creating the base software and tools for chips containing Armv8 processors", developers spent "another 1.5 billion hours creating their apps and software", and Arm expected to invest "more than 30 million hours" on Armv9 (424B4 2023). The same document gives the honest base rate for building an ecosystem from scratch: "we started to invest in the technology and ecosystem for the server market in 2007, but it took over 10 years for Arm to start to gain share, given the lack of an existing hardware/software ecosystem at the start."

Arm raises price per unit independently of volume. In the September-2024 quarter smartphone royalty grew 40% against what the CFO described as a mid-single-digit increase in handsets sold; in the June-2025 quarter royalty grew about 50% on mid-single-digit unit growth; and in the June-2026 quarter, with the smartphone market projected down by double digits, Arm projected double-digit growth in smartphone royalties (Q2 FY2025, Q1 FY2026 and Q1 FY2027 calls). Nearly half of one year's royalty comes from designs licensed more than a decade earlier — "approximately 46% of our royalty revenue for the fiscal year ended March 31, 2023 came from products released between 1990 to 2012" (424B4 2023), and roughly 50% of current royalty comes from products launched more than ten years ago [deck: Q3 FYE26 2026-02-04, slide 21]. Very few technology assets amortise that slowly.

The CPU microarchitectures themselves are demonstrably substitutable inside Arm's own architecture. The proof is Arm's largest customers. Apple, Qualcomm via Nuvia, Amazon with Graviton, Fujitsu, Ampere and NVIDIA all hold Architecture License Agreements and design their own Arm-ISA CPUs. They replaced Arm's cores and kept paying Arm. NVIDIA's Vera, in production in the June-2026 quarter, uses 88 NVIDIA-designed Olympus cores in place of Grace's 72 licensed Neoverse V2 cores (The Register, accessed 2026-08-03) [web]. What none of them replaced was the software base attached to the ISA. Where Arm competes on the standard its position has survived thirty-five years of attack; where it competes on the design it competes with Apple's, Amazon's, NVIDIA's and Qualcomm's CPU teams.

Compute Subsystems are the commercial expression of the second competence. A CSS is a pre-integrated, pre-verified configuration of CPU, GPU, NPU, interconnect and system IP, delivered as RTL or as GDSII already physically implemented for a named foundry process (20-F FY2026, Item 4.B). What it replaces is the licensee's own integration, physical implementation and verification work, and the CEO quantified the saving: "in some cases, it shaves a year, in some cases, 18 months off the time of starting design to get into production" (Arm Everywhere, 2026-03-24). Two barriers protect it. The physical implementation is node-specific and must be redone for each foundry process, which is headcount. And a CSS is only worth buying if the buyer already trusts the IP inside it — a merchant CPU vendor cannot sell one because it does not license IP, and an IP vendor without Arm's ecosystem cannot sell one because nobody needs the integration if they were not going to use the cores anyway. The firms that could attack it — Cadence, Synopsys, Alphawave, the custom-ASIC arms of Broadcom and Marvell — all offer subsystem and full-chip services; none can put an Arm CPU inside at a royalty rate they set.

The evidence CSS differentiates is renewal behaviour and price. "Every CSS customer that's had a chance to sign up for the next version or kind of renew for the next generation has all done that" (Q3 FY2026 call, CFO). The rate is roughly double Armv9's, and escalates within CSS: "the royalty rate on a CSS of '26 is not necessarily the same as the royalty rate for CSS in '25 ... those are not onetime increases in royalty rate" (Q3 FY2025 call). The disclosed per-core pricing ladder runs approximately 1.0x for first-generation Armv9 (2023 launches), 1.5x for subsequent-generation Armv9 (2025), 2.0x for first-generation CSS and 3.0x for subsequent-generation CSS [deck: Q1 FYE27 2026-07-29, slide 20]. Design wins are real: MediaTek's Dimensity 9400 was the first CSS in mobile silicon, Microsoft's Azure Cobalt 100 and 200 are built on Neoverse CSS, and NVIDIA's RTX Spark is built on Arm compute subsystems.

The countervailing pressure is contractual and disclosed. "Certain of our contracts with key customers contain provisions allowing such customers to obtain licenses to our latest products as soon as they are made available to any other customer" (20-F FY2026, Item 3.D). A most-favoured-access right is the contractual opposite of price discrimination on the newest CSS.

Neoverse and the data-centre software ecosystem. Neoverse launched in 2019 after twelve years of investment. It is not technically exotic; what makes it work is that a hyperscaler can license it, or license the architecture, and build a CPU tuned to its own workload and power envelope instead of buying a merchant part specified for everyone. Arm's EVP of the Cloud AI business described the mechanism directly: "Arm has been investing in data center software ecosystem for well over 15 years ... And then something happened in 2019. We launched Arm Neoverse ... it allowed our customers to begin to launch products with a much lower barrier to entry ... And that started that software flywheel turning" (Arm Everywhere, 2026-03-24). Cumulative Neoverse cores took six years to reach the first billion and nine months to add the most recent half-billion.

The software layer has three pieces. KleidiAI provides hand-optimised kernels so that an AI framework automatically uses Armv9 vector and matrix instructions, integrated natively into PyTorch, ExecuTorch, ONNX Runtime, XNNPACK, llama.cpp, LiteRT and Alibaba MNN, delivering acceleration "without code changes" (arm.com/technologies/sme2, accessed 2026-08-03) [web]. Arm is contributing server-ready, authenticated-access and diagnostic standards to the Open Compute Project, and was explicit that these "won't just be for the Arm AGI CPU, they will apply to the entire ecosystem". And the developer base grew from more than 15 million at IPO to more than 22 million at FY2026 close, roughly 47% in three years (424B4 2023; 20-F FY2026, Item 4.B).

GPU, NPU and System IP are the weak leg, and Arm discloses nothing about them. Arm sells GPUs, NPUs and system IP and treats them as complements — "We offer a family of GPU and NPU products providing efficient computing acceleration" (20-F FY2026, Item 4.B). Arm reports no attach rate, revenue or share for any of them. Every quantified royalty-rate uplift Arm publishes is attributed to Armv9 and to CSS. Arm also sold its Artisan foundation IP business — standard cell libraries, memory compilers and general-purpose I/O — to Cadence in August 2025. In mobile, Qualcomm ships Adreno, Apple ships its own GPU and Imagination is still shipping. What the accelerators demonstrably contribute is CSS content, because a CSS bundles CPU, GPU, NPU and system IP and the royalty rate is set on the bundle. That is a legitimate role. It is not evidence of technical leadership, and because Arm publishes nothing, the risk that GPU attach erosion is a per-chip rate headwind running against the Armv9 and CSS tailwind can be named but cannot be sized. Section 20 lists that gap.

Substitution threats. RISC-V is the only architectural substitute with momentum, and Arm names it: "We face significant competition from established technologies such as the x86 architecture, as well as from free, open-source technologies, including the RISC-V architecture. Some of our customers are also major supporters of the RISC-V architecture" (20-F FY2026, Item 4.B). Two leading indicators point in opposite directions and both are recent. Google removed RISC-V support from the Android Generic Kernel Image in May 2024, which means a RISC-V Android build requires a vendor-maintained kernel branch (The Register, accessed 2026-08-03) [web]; that is the software barrier working as Arm describes it. Against that, NVIDIA announced it is bringing CUDA platform support to RISC-V, letting a RISC-V CPU act as the host processor in a CUDA system, a role previously restricted to x86 and Arm (riscv.org, accessed 2026-08-03) [web]. No ship date has been announced and it depends on data-centre-class RISC-V silicon existing.

The 20-F names a second substitution vector: "our competitors may be able to use AI tools to quickly convert software written for the Arm ISA to competing ISAs, reducing the cost to enter markets where we are already established and resulting in increased competition" (20-F FY2026, Item 3.D). Arm's entire moat is the cost of porting software. If that cost approaches zero, so does the moat. Arm supplied the counter-evidence itself: it brought Meta's Paul Saab on stage at Arm Everywhere specifically to prove the ecosystem argument, and he testified that Meta's initial full port took "about 90 days, 5 engineers", and that "if I were starting the port today, I would be using an LLM ... the barrier to entry today like porting to Arm is, I would say, close to 0." He was arguing the moat is cheap to cross into.

Finally, x86 is not standing still. AMD's Zen 6 EPYC "Venice" reaches 256 cores on TSMC 2 nm and Intel's Xeon 6+ "Clearwater Forest" reaches 288 Darkmont efficiency cores on 18A, both in the 2026 window [web, Tom's Hardware and The Register, accessed 2026-08-03]. Arm's own "more than 2x performance per rack" comparison is drawn against a 128-core-per-socket x86 configuration — 17 two-socket 2U servers holding 4,352 cores — which is a 2024-generation part, and every supporting chart is labelled "based on estimates", normalised, and run with simultaneous multithreading disabled on both sides (Arm Everywhere keynote, slides 51–57). No third-party benchmark of the AGI CPU exists.

The enforcement mechanism, and the case Arm lost. The Architecture Licence Agreement is what converts a published specification into a rent, and in 2022 Arm tested how strong that contract is. It sued Qualcomm and Nuvia over the assignment of Nuvia's ALA. The outcome, from Arm's own filing: "The jury concluded that certain technology was licensed to Qualcomm under the Qualcomm license and that Qualcomm had not breached the Nuvia ALA but failed to reach a verdict on whether Nuvia breached the Nuvia ALA. ... On September 30, 2025, the Court affirmed the jury verdict ... and granted Qualcomm judgment as a matter of law in its favor to conclude that Nuvia did not breach the Nuvia ALA" (20-F FY2026, Item 5). Arm has appealed to the Third Circuit. Standing law is that a company can acquire an Arm architecture licensee, absorb its CPU design work, and continue shipping under its own pre-existing licence. That is the exact manoeuvre Arm tried to block.

3. Competitive advantages & moat assessment

Arm's position in the CPU architecture is a real structural advantage and an unusually durable one, but it is a weaker economic asset than its market position implies, and it is being defended by escalating reinvestment, not converted into cash returns. The advantage is moderate: it earns excess returns today and is defensible for now, materially above a commodity position and well short of a business that throws off excess returns without heavy spending to hold the ground. Two erosion vectors are already visible in the numbers, and a third — Arm competing with its own licensees — has just been created deliberately.

Three facts establish that the advantage is real.

  • The switching cost is not borne by the party that pays it. A licensee that abandons the Arm instruction set does not lose Arm's design work; it loses compatibility with the software its own customers already run. Arm holds above 99% of mobile applications processors "by virtue of all key mobile operating systems depending on Arm processors" (20-F FY2026, Item 4.B), and that share has not moved across three successive 20-Fs despite every large licensee having both the capital and the motive to leave.
  • The annuity is very long. Roughly 50% of current royalty revenue comes from products launched more than ten years ago, and Arm is still collecting on designs from the early 1990s [deck: Q4 FYE26 2026-05-06, slide 30].
  • Per-unit pricing power is demonstrated in a market whose prices normally decline. In the quarter ended 2026-06-30 Arm's royalty grew 22.2% to $715m while Qualcomm's QTL licensing revenue — levied on the same handset base in the same quarter — declined 3.0% to $1,278m (Arm 6-K 2026-Q2, Note 3; QCOM 10-Q for the quarter ended 2026-06-28).

Three facts establish that it is not a top-tier business.

  • The reinvestment burden is extreme. FY2026 R&D of $2,776m exceeded the entire royalty line of $2,613m, and R&D intensity rose from 36.8% of revenue in FY2022 to 56.4% in FY2026 and 65.0% in the latest quarter.
  • Returns do not show up. GAAP ROIC was 16.3% in FY2026 against 28.3% in FY2023, and the three-year incremental return on invested capital (FY2023 to FY2026) was 6.4% — $181m of incremental after-tax operating profit on $2,849m of incremental invested capital, below any reasonable cost of capital for an all-equity, high-beta business.
  • Value capture has not expanded. Arm's take of the value of the chips built on its architecture was approximately 1.9% in FYE21 and approximately 1.9% in FYE25 [estimate: royalty revenue divided by Arm-based chip value read from Arm's own market-share chart; ±10% error in reading the chart puts both endpoints in a 1.7%–2.1% range]. Royalty growth over that period came from Arm-based chip value growing, not from Arm taking a larger cut of it.

ROIC = GAAP operating income × (1 − 21%) ÷ average invested capital. Invested capital = total shareholders' equity + total debt − cash and cash equivalents − short-term investments. Arm has no debt. ROIIC (3-year) = (NOPAT in year t − NOPAT in year t−3) ÷ (invested capital at t − invested capital at t−3). A normalised 21% tax rate is used because the reported effective rate was a 44.3% benefit in FY2024 and a 10.0% benefit in FY2025.

Management claims: the ecosystem ("more than 22 million developers ... the breadth of the Arm ecosystem creates a virtuous cycle that deeply integrates us into the design cycle"); close integration with customers' R&D functions two to three years before tape-out; cost-sharing ("we can typically cover the entire cost of developing new Arm products by charging each customer only a portion of the total development costs"); and long-term visibility from the royalty stream (20-F FY2026, Item 4.B, Competitive Strengths). Note what the last two say about pricing: Arm's stated model is cost amortisation across many licensees, and the stated source of visibility is the royalty, not the licence. The 20-F claims no licence-fee pricing power.

External sources are thin on licence economics and heavy on royalty; no Arm-primary sell-side report was available, so no analyst thesis is quoted. What external sources do supply is the counter-evidence. Arm lost the Nuvia case at trial and on judgment as a matter of law and is appealing (The Register, accessed 2026-08-03) [web]. Qualcomm acquired Ventana Micro Systems in December 2025, bringing high-performance RISC-V data-centre CPU design in-house alongside its custom Arm-based Oryon cores (Qualcomm, accessed 2026-08-03) [web]. And the US Federal Trade Commission opened an antitrust probe in 2026 examining "whether Arm sought to illegally monopolize parts of the semiconductor market by refusing to provide central processing unit design licenses or by degrading their quality", with reporting tying it to the March 2026 AGI CPU launch [web, Tom's Hardware and TechTimes, accessed 2026-08-03; the Tom's Hardware article body was paywalled on fetch and the substance is corroborated across secondary coverage]. Widely circulated third-party claims that RISC-V has already reached "25% global market share" trace only to low-credibility syndication and are not relied on here.

Ground-up view: four features of the industry determine who earns rent. The unit of lock-in is software, not silicon, and the party bearing the switching cost is the licensee's customer, who owns compiled binaries, developer skills, toolchains and operating-system dependencies — which is why fourteen years of well-funded attack by customers who would each save real money has not moved the share. Minimum efficient scale is set by R&D amortisation: Arm spends $2,776m a year across an installed base shipping roughly 31 billion chips, and a licensee replicating that roadmap for its own volume amortises the same engineering over one to two orders of magnitude fewer units. That is a cost barrier, not a legal bar, and licensees with enough volume clear it — which is precisely what the architecture licence exists to permit. The price is set low enough to be uninteresting to attack: roughly $0.89 per smartphone applications processor, under 0.3% of a $300–$400 handset, and roughly 1.8% of the value of Arm-based chip content. The low take is the defence, and it is also why 99% share does not translate into Visa-like returns. And the base rate deflates by contract: rates step down as cumulative volume rises, so holding per-chip revenue flat requires selling a higher rung — Armv8 to Armv9 to CSS — every generation. Growth in royalty per chip is product substitution at a higher list rate, which is a weaker form of pricing power than a business that can simply raise price, and it is the reason R&D intensity has to keep rising.

Buyer power is high, concentrated and individually unusable. The top five customers including Arm China and SoftBank were 57% of FY2026 revenue, 56% in FY2025 and 54% in FY2024; Arm China alone was 16%, 17% and 21%. Arm concedes the consequence: customer concentration "has afforded certain customers significant bargaining power, which has, in some cases, resulted in pricing or other contractual terms that are less favorable to us" (20-F FY2026, Item 3.D). No individual buyer can exercise that power without stranding its own software base, which is why the concentration shows up in rate negotiation.

3.2 Business-model analogues

Qualcomm's QTL segment is the closest by shape: a per-unit levy on nearly every handset shipped, priced small enough that designing around it is not worth the effort, protected by a position the payer's customers depend on. It holds because both businesses tax the same units. It breaks because QTL depends on patents that expire and on FRAND rate constraints, whereas Arm's depends on a software base that does not expire — visible in the divergence above. Qualcomm is also the analogue in a second sense: Arm now sells silicon into markets its licensees serve, which is Qualcomm's permanent structure, and Qualcomm is the party running the antitrust campaign against Arm that it once had run against itself.

Dolby Laboratories is the same shape one cycle later: a per-unit device levy on a de facto standard, 88.1% gross margin, R&D at 19.4% of revenue. It holds as the template for what this model looks like at maturity, and it is the cautionary version — Dolby's revenue went from $1,281m (FY2021) to $1,349m (FY2025) because it has no equivalent of the Armv9-to-CSS ladder to sell, and its operating margin declined from 45–50% in 2008–2009 to the high teens today, not because a competitor displaced it but because the device base stopped growing while it kept spending. Arm's licensee unit base, at above 99% share of a contracting smartphone market, is at the same structural position.

Cadence Design Systems shares the switching-cost mechanism: qualified into a multi-year chip design flow, priced as a portfolio subscription, with IP sold alongside tools. It breaks in one specific and measurable way — Cadence's contracted forward book is 1.47x annual revenue against Arm's 0.42x, and none of Cadence's comes from its controlling shareholder.

S&P Global and Moody's are the right reference class for the "the customer's whole workflow is built on it" logic, but the analogy breaks on reinvestment: those businesses do not spend more than their own royalty revenue on R&D to hold position. Arm is stronger than Dolby on growth and stronger than QTL on trajectory; it is weaker than all of them on returns on capital, and materially weaker than Visa, Google and Meta on the reinvestment required to hold the ground. The structural facts underlying this assessment — market shares, concentration and who sets price — are established in Section 4.

4. Competitive landscape & market sizing

Arm holds above 99% of mobile applications processors "for many years" (20-F FY2026, Item 4.B), close to 100% of data-centre DPUs and SmartNICs (Q4 FY26 call, CFO), and about 50% of CPU compute among top hyperscalers (Q4 FYE26 shareholder letter). Those three figures are Arm's, on Arm's definitions, and the third has no defined denominator — cores, sockets, vCPU-hours and spend give materially different answers, and Arm has not said which it means. 4.2 Market sizing, and where the TAM slide overstates the addressable part

Arm's own FYE26 total addressed semiconductor content is $535bn, of which Cloud AI is $330bn ($35bn other CPU + $50bn data-centre CPU + $245bn accelerators), Edge AI $180bn and Physical AI $25bn [deck: Q4 FYE26 2026-05-06, slide 14]. The FYE31 figure is more than $1.5tn. Arm's own March-2026 deck gave a FYE26 total of $415bn six weeks earlier, and its component slide summed to $325bn against a stated Cloud AI total of $210bn on the facing page — the March deck is internally inconsistent and the May restatement is 29% higher with no explanatory note. And the accelerator block, $245bn of the FYE26 figure and more than $1tn by FYE31, is silicon Arm earns almost nothing on: the Arm content on a GPU or TPU die is de minimis.

The consequence is that the layer Arm sells into is losing share of the AI silicon dollar on Arm's own estimates. Data-centre CPU is $50bn of $330bn of cloud-AI silicon in FYE26 (15.2%) and more than $100bn of more than $1.2tn in FYE31 (8.3%). Absolute CPU royalty can compound at 20% while Arm's share of the AI compute dollar falls by roughly half.

Building the purchasable market bottom-up gives a smaller number than the TAM slide. Intel disclosed DCAI revenue of $5,052m in Q1 CY2026 of which "Other DCAI product revenue was $935 million", leaving $4,117m of server CPU revenue (INTC 10-Q 2026-Q1). Grossing that up by Mercury's Intel x86 revenue share of 53.8% gives a total x86 server CPU market of $7,652m in the quarter, roughly $30.6bn annualised, rising to about $39.5bn on the Q2 rate. The merchant x86 server CPU market is therefore running at $31–40bn a year, and the Arm-architecture portion of Arm's $50bn TAM is mostly captive silicon — Graviton, Axion, Cobalt, Grace and Vera — that never transacts at a merchant price. Arm's TAM is not inflated in aggregate; what it obscures is how little of it a merchant part can address.

For calibration on the outer year, sell-side 2030 data-centre CPU market estimates run: AMD more than $120bn, UBS $170bn, Citi $132bn, BofA $125bn, Arm more than $100bn [web, io-fund compilation, accessed 2026-08-03]. Arm's is the lowest of the five. The aggressive assumption in Arm's plan is the share, not the market size. $15bn of $100bn is roughly 15% of the entire data-centre CPU market by FYE31, from zero in FY2026. For comparison, AMD launched EPYC in 2017 and needed until Q1 2026 — nine years — to reach 46.2% of x86 server CPU revenue and 27.4% of total server CPU units, with a mature x86 software base, an established OEM channel and a direct price-performance comparison.

On the licensing side Arm dominates a small pool. The merchant design-IP market was $8.492bn in 2024, up 20.2%, with the top four suppliers at 75% of it (eeNews Europe / IPnest, accessed 2026-08-03) [web]. Arm's calendar-2024 revenue rebuilt from its own disclosed fiscal quarters was $3,694m, or 43.5% of that market [estimate], and the same answer comes independently from IPnest's Arm-plus-Synopsys share of 66% less Synopsys Design IP revenue of $1,906m, giving 43.6%. Narrowing to processor IP, where Arm competes, a bottom-up sum of the named vendors puts the pool at roughly $5.6bn and Arm at roughly 83% of it, or 79% excluding the SoftBank consulting stream [estimate]. Arm's Q1 FY2027 licence line of $574m is 1.26x Synopsys Design IP's whole quarter and 2.4x Cadence's entire semiconductor-IP business. No competitor is within reach of that revenue. Arm's own slide states the ceiling on this pool plainly: the FYE26 Cloud AI IP/CSS opportunity is $2.4bn against $24bn of whole-chip value on the same silicon [deck: Arm Everywhere investor session 2026-03-24, slide 37]. The licensing model captures roughly a tenth of the value of the chips it enables in its fastest-growing end market, and no amount of share gain inside the licensing pool can close that.

4.4 Share counter-evidence, ranked

Customers moving from Arm-designed cores to Arm-ISA custom cores is the largest quantified threat and it is happening in Arm's highest-growth, highest-value sockets. NVIDIA's Vera replaced 72 Neoverse V2 cores with 88 in-house Olympus cores; Qualcomm ships Oryon across phones, PCs and now data centre; Apple has never used an Arm-designed core in an A or M series part. Arm's own risk factor states the consequence: customers "may decide to license our ISA and develop their own processors based on our ISA, rather than utilize our predeveloped products through an implementation license, resulting in less fees paid to us" (20-F FY2026, Item 3.D). Arm keeps the socket and the ecosystem; it loses the implementation uplift that is the entire source of its royalty-per-chip growth. How much it loses is not knowable from disclosure: the published per-core price ladder has no architecture-licence rung on it at all, so the mix effect inside the fastest-growing driver is signed but not sized.

The smartphone unit base is contracting. 43% of royalty sits on a market whose chipset shipments declined 15% in the first half of CY2026, with memory prices not expected to normalise before the second half of CY2027 [web]. Arm's own multi-year royalty growth expectation was cut from around 20% to the high teens as a direct result.

RISC-V in embedded and automotive. Free, and backed by Arm's own customers — the 20-F names the August 2023 automotive RISC-V joint venture formed by "a group of our customers and other competitors" (Quintauris: Bosch, Infineon, Nordic, NXP, Qualcomm). Third-party forecasts put RISC-V at roughly a quarter of processor shipments by 2030, growing about 50% a year from 2024 (Omdia, accessed 2026-08-03) [web]. Those are unit forecasts, not revenue: the named RISC-V vendors' combined licensing revenue is roughly $250–350m against Arm's $4.7bn. The near-term threat is not RISC-V revenue; it is RISC-V as the reason a Flexible Access prospect never signs. Arm Flexible Access net customer additions — the on-ramp for start-ups and new business units, exactly the RISC-V recruiting ground — declined from +92 in FY2025 to +15 in FY2026, down 84%, in a year when design activity was rising. Arm retired the metric one quarter later.

Workloads migrating from CPU to accelerator. Arm's own risk factor: "if, over time, more of the workloads move to accelerators away from the CPU, the CPU market size may decrease, resulting in a decrease of our license fees and royalties" (20-F FY2026, Item 3.D). Arm's FYE26 TAM puts accelerators at $245bn against $85bn for all CPUs, and forecasts accelerators at more than $1tn by FYE31 against $155bn of CPU.

Arm competing with its own licensees. New language in the FY2026 20-F: "many of our customers who have historically licensed our IP may face direct competition from us in certain market segments ... these customers or partners may terminate or materially reduce their relationship with us, seek alternative architectures or products, develop their own proprietary architectures, withhold sensitive roadmap information, reduce collaboration on product development, challenge our pricing models or demand more favorable commercial terms, or pursue legal action" (Item 3.D). The AGI CPU's named lead partner, Meta, signed a multi-year agreement with AWS in April 2026 to deploy tens of millions of Graviton5 cores (aboutamazon.com, accessed 2026-08-03) [web] and a multi-generational agreement with Qualcomm for CPUs for its future server fleet, production beginning in the second half of 2028 [web, cloudnews.tech, accessed 2026-08-03] — the same window in which AGI CPU generations 2 and 3 would ship. And SoftBank, which owns 86.4% of Arm, bought Ampere outright for $6.5bn in November 2025, four months before Arm launched a merchant Arm server CPU into the same market. Arm has not disclosed how the two are positioned relative to each other.

Merchant IP supply is consolidating out of the market. Qualcomm absorbed Alphawave, the fourth-ranked design-IP supplier, in December 2025 for roughly $2.4bn enterprise value [web]; Synopsys is exiting processor IP; Cadence bought Arm's own Artisan business; and GlobalFoundries assembled MIPS and Synopsys ARC into a foundry-owned CPU-IP business that can price IP at zero against wafer commitments [web, gf.com, accessed 2026-08-03]. That raises Arm's measured share while reducing the number of independent firms whose survival would validate the standalone IP-licensing model.

5. Value chain & profit pools

Arm is in two chains that behave differently, plus one flow that is not a value chain at all.

Chain A, edge and smartphone, is 43% of royalty. Arm licenses CPU IP to an SoC vendor, which has the die fabricated at TSMC or Samsung, packaged at an OSAT, combined with DRAM and NAND from Micron, SK Hynix or Samsung, and sold to a handset maker. Arm's royalty is a percentage of the chip price or a fixed fee per chip, never a percentage of the handset.

Chain B, cloud and data centre, is the fastest-growing part of royalty. Arm licenses Neoverse IP or a Neoverse CSS to a hyperscaler or merchant vendor, the die is fabricated at TSMC, packaged with advanced packaging and memory, integrated into a rack alongside accelerators, and deployed. From end-calendar-2026 Arm also intends to sell a finished CPU into this chain itself.

5.3 The binding constraint, and where it is mis-located

The CEO named the constraint: "You have wafers, you have substrates, you have test capacity, you have memory and all of those areas our confidence level and being able to secure the supply necessary has gotten better" (Q1 FY27 call). Arm had supply for $1bn against disclosed demand above $2bn at Q4 FY2026, and at Q1 FY2027 had "secured the manufacturing capacity needed to support the $1 billion opportunity" with demand still above $2bn. Roughly half the disclosed demand remains unsupplied.

Arm is a marginal buyer in the pool it now needs. NVIDIA holds $21.4bn of inventory and $95.2bn of inventory purchase and long-term supply and capacity obligations; Micron's 2026 capex is guided above $25bn; TSMC's 2026 capex is guided at $52–56bn. Arm's disclosed semiconductor-related purchase commitment is approximately $100m — a ratio of roughly 1:950 against NVIDIA — with no fabs, no disclosed long-term wafer agreement, no disclosed prepayments and no inventory line at 2026-06-30. NVIDIA, the largest buyer in the pool, states that lead times are "extended beyond twelve months for some products", that it has "paid premiums, provided deposits, and entered into long-term supply agreements and capacity commitments", and that it "may still be unable to secure sufficient commitments for capacity to address our business needs" (NVDA 10-Q, quarter ended 2026-04-26). Arm also buys wafers through an unnamed ASIC design partner for the first generation, so a third party currently stands between Arm and the foundry. Arm is not without leverage — it is the architecture supplier to most of the parties competing for the same capacity, and its volumes are tiny relative to a single month of N3 output — but it has no contractual claim on allocation that it has disclosed.

Memory constrains Arm chiefly through demand, by pricing handsets out of the market, and only secondarily through supply, because the supply-constrained business is 0% of revenue today and roughly 12% of it in FY2028. The demand channel is already visible in the royalty series: +25%, +21%, +27%, +11% across FY2026, +22% in Q1 FY2027, guided +13% for Q2 FY2027.

5.4 Customer-side economics

At $0.89 per smartphone on a $450 wholesale device and roughly 0.15%–0.5% of an AI rack, Arm's royalty is not a line item a customer optimises against on price alone. That is real protection against RISC-V displacement on cost grounds, and it is also the reason Arm must change what it sells, not what it charges, to raise capture.

The AGI CPU customer-return claim does not survive arithmetic in the form it is made. Arm states the chip "will deliver more than 2x performance per rack compared with x86-based platforms, which allows the AI data center capital expenditure to be reduced by up to $10 billion per gigawatt" (Q4 FYE26 shareholder letter). Take Arm's own data-centre CPU market of more than $100bn by 2030 against roughly 20 GW of new data-centre capacity a year at the 2030 run-rate [web], and CPU spend per gigawatt is roughly $5bn; total data-centre capex is put at roughly $67bn per gigawatt on the same forecasts [web]. A $10bn-per-gigawatt saving is therefore twice the entire annual CPU spend per gigawatt implied by Arm's own market size, and about 15% of all-in capex per gigawatt. It cannot be a saving on CPU purchases. It can only hold if it aggregates power, cooling, floor space, network and accelerator-adjacent savings from a denser rack. Arm does not show the bridge, and the figure is footnoted "based on Arm internal estimates". The narrower claims are better supported and do not need it: NVIDIA's Vera is stated to deliver up to 50% higher CPU performance and 2x greater energy efficiency than comparable x86 systems, and independent inspection put the AGI CPU at 2.2 W per core against 3.9 W for a 128-core Granite Rapids Xeon and 2.3 W for a 144-core Sierra Forest part (The Next Platform, accessed 2026-08-03) [web].

Every named AGI CPU customer already has an Arm CPU option. AWS has Graviton5 at 192 cores, Google has Axion, Microsoft has Cobalt, NVIDIA has Vera. Arm's named buyer list — Meta, Cloudflare, Cerebras, OpenAI, SAP, F5, SK Telecom, Positron, Rebellions, Verda, Oracle — skews toward organisations without an in-house CPU programme, which is how the CEO defines the segment: "customers who simply aren't going to design their own Arm-based CPU either for CapEx reasons or engineering reasons" (Q4 FY26 call). The product's economics for the customer are an Arm CPU without the design team, and its competitive set is Ampere — now a SoftBank sister company — and merchant x86.

5.5 Bargaining power, and the direction it is moving

Arm's suppliers are gaining bargaining power. Arm's EDA liabilities went from $87m to $175m in a year, up 101%, against R&D up 34%, in the same year it sold its Artisan foundation IP business to Cadence: Arm now buys more from the EDA layer and sells it less, to counterparties running 86.4% and 77.0% gross margins. Arm has $1,056.5m of non-cancelable purchase obligations for cloud computing, data centres, software and services running to 2036, and added a further $305.0m through 2029 in April 2026 — $1,362m in total, equal to 27.7% of FY2026 revenue and 49% of FY2026 R&D, owed to a supplier that is also a customer. Foundry and memory are gaining and Arm is a new, small buyer.

Arm's realized price per unit of content is rising, and Arm cannot reprice into an inflating chain. More than 70% of forecast royalties are on rates already under contract — roughly 100% for FYE27, 85% FYE28, 75% FYE29, 65% FYE30, 60% FYE31 [deck: Arm Everywhere investor session 2026-03-24, slide 46] — and the rate steps down as cumulative volume rises. Arm's suppliers reprice annually or on allocation. Realized royalty rises only through mix migration, never through repricing. That asymmetry is the structural weakness of the licensing layer in an input-cost shock, and it is why a memory shock reaches Arm as a volume loss with no price offset.

Downstream of Arm's customers, server OEMs are losing: Dell's ISG grew AI-optimised server revenue from $1,873m to $24,683m across three years while segment operating margin went 12.6% → 12.8% → 11.7%. Cloud operators are holding at a 35.4% operating margin. Handset makers are losing pricing discretion to memory, with ASPs forced up roughly $100 to a record $550 while units decline 13.9%.

5.6 Circular and related-party flows, sized

Nine flows connect Arm to its controlling shareholder and its own ecosystem, and together they are the most distinctive feature of this chain.

  • The parent buys roughly 15% of Arm's revenue. The Consulting Agreement produced $145.5m (FY2025), $704.4m (FY2026) and $192.9m (Q1 FY2027), guided at roughly $200m a quarter for the rest of FY2027.
  • More than half of it is unbilled. Cumulative revenue recognised is $1,042.8m; related-party contract assets were $645.8m at 2026-03-31 and $577.2m at 2026-06-30, so 55% of everything ever recognised under the agreement is still an unbilled contract asset. One statement of work was restructured into a fixed $300m payment due during FY2027, less than half the balance.
  • Strip it out and the licensing business shrank, as set out in §1.1.
  • A competitor became a sister company. SoftBank acquired 100% of Ampere in November 2025. Arm now sells to Ampere ($5.4m in Q1 FY2027) and buys development services from it under a March-2026 Ampere Development Agreement ($5.0m of expense in Q1 FY2027), and collected $39.3m in December 2025 settling an Ampere convertible loan.
  • The parent's stake is levered against Arm's share price. SoftBank subsidiaries borrowed $8.5bn secured by a pledge of 769,029,000 Arm ordinary shares, 72.0% of the equity as of 2026-05-21, with prepayment triggers "in the event the trading price of our ADSs declines below certain thresholds" and possible margin calls (20-F FY2026, Item 3.D). Arm has no material obligations under it, so this is a control-and-supply-of-stock exposure, not a balance-sheet one.
  • Arm is taking principal risk on third-party silicon supply through the roughly $100m arrangement described in §1.4, with exposure Arm itself enumerates as "third-party default, order cancellation, periods during which we bear financial exposure without corresponding customer cash flows, and related inventory risks".
  • Arm's compute supplier is also its customer: $1,362m of committed cloud and data-centre purchases to an unnamed provider, in a market where AWS, Azure and Google Cloud are simultaneously Arm's fastest-growing royalty customers.
  • Arm holds equity in ecosystem participants: 10% non-voting in Acetone (whose main asset is 48.2% of Arm China, Arm's largest customer), a further $67.9m of Raspberry Pi shares bought in April 2026, and a fully reserved $3.2m loan to Cerfe Labs.
  • Arm China is a customer, not a subsidiary, and Arm depends on Arm China for the financial information used to compute Arm's own revenue.

6. Secular & industry trends

Arm publishes revenue by customer headquarters, and that disaggregation runs against the Cloud AI attribution used in Sections 1 and 4. US-headquartered revenue grew 2.6% in FY2026, from $1,716m to $1,761m, contributing 4.9% of the $913m increase, while Japan rose 178.7% from $296m to $825m and supplied 57.9% of it (20-F FY2026, Note 4). In the June-2026 quarter US revenue grew 1.6% ($382m to $388m) and PRC revenue declined 4.3% ($209m to $200m) while total revenue grew 22.4% and Japan grew 79%. Every named customer behind the Cloud AI growth — Amazon, Google, Microsoft, Meta, NVIDIA, Qualcomm, Apple — is US-headquartered, and the domain attribution below puts $149m of FY2026 royalty growth against a $45m increase in total US revenue. Either the Cloud AI attribution is overstated, or other US revenue declined by roughly $100m and that decline is undescribed.

6.1 Which cycle Arm tracks

Not the semiconductor dollar cycle. WSTS puts the 2025 semiconductor market at $772bn, +22%, and its Spring 2026 forecast at $1.51tn for 2026, +90%, with memory up roughly 250% to more than $800bn [web, wsts.org, accessed 2026-08-03]. Arm has no exposure to the memory pricing producing that boom: its royalty is a percentage of the CPU chip's price or a fixed fee, and it licenses no memory IP. Comparing Arm to "semiconductor industry growth" is a category error in both directions.

Not the wafer-volume cycle either, though it is closer. SEMI reports 2025 silicon wafer shipments of 12,973 million square inches, +5.8%, then +13.1% year over year in Q1 2026 and +7.4% in Q2 [web, semi.org, accessed 2026-08-03]. Arm's royalty grew 21% in FY2026 against a wafer-area base growing 6%. The fifteen-point gap is content, not volume.

What Arm tracks is a weighted blend of end-device unit cycles, currently about 43% smartphones, a fast-growing data-centre slice and a tail of microcontroller, consumer and automotive units. The two halves are now moving in opposite directions: smartphone units are forecast down 13.9% in CY2026 on memory-led BOM inflation, while data-centre royalty has more than doubled year over year for three consecutive quarters.

The reporting lag matters for reading the quarters. Royalty reports received between April and March relate to shipments made in the prior calendar year (424B4 2023), so royalty in a quarter reflects licensee shipments in the prior quarter. Q1 FY2027 royalty reflects chips shipped January to March 2026, before the handset build-plan cuts. Q2 FY2027 is the first quarter to include them, which is why it is guided to +13% against +22% delivered.

Against Arm's own ultimate volume driver. 6.3 What steepens and what flattens the slope

Steepening. Agentic AI raising CPU intensity per data-centre watt is the claim the whole silicon case depends on: roughly 30 million CPU cores per gigawatt today rising to roughly 120 million once agentic workloads dominate, because agents issue more than 15x the requests of humans and run continuously, so CPU orchestration becomes the bottleneck ahead of accelerator throughput [deck: Arm Everywhere keynote 2026-03-24, slides 22–31]. The keynote illustrates the mechanism, showing a request queue backing up with GPUs idle at 0% utilisation. The 4x figure is Arm's own internal estimate, footnoted as such, with no third-party corroboration in the record, and the CEO acknowledged that before Arm published $100bn "most folks were talking about a number of around 50 to 60" (Q1 FY27 call). Since March 2026 several third-party 2030 data-centre CPU estimates have come in above Arm's — UBS $170bn, Citi $132bn, BofA $125bn, AMD more than $120bn [web] — so the market size is corroborated even where the cores-per-gigawatt step is not. And the realized doubling of Arm's data-centre royalty reflects hyperscalers adopting Arm-based custom CPUs in place of x86, which is a share shift, not a demonstrated increase in cores per unit of capacity. The two are being conflated.

Host-CPU displacement inside accelerator racks is real and has a definable ceiling: Google's TPU8t and TPU8i replace x86 host processors with Arm-based Axion, NVIDIA's Vera is in production, Cobalt 200 and Graviton5 are scaling. One host CPU complex per accelerator tray is a socket-for-socket substitution, not an open-ended market. DPUs and SmartNICs, where Arm has close to 100% share, scale with network ports, not servers. And CSS behaves as an annual price escalator: "Every smartphone cycle, we deliver a brand-new CSS. Each time we deliver the brand-new CSS, the royalty rates are generally increased year-on-year" (Q3 FY2026 call, CEO).

Flattening. Memory-driven handset unit destruction is the immediate one. On Arm's own published elasticity — a 20% unit decline costing "at most a kind of 4% to 6% revenue impact, just specifically within smartphones" (Q3 FY2026 call, CFO) — a 13.9% unit year costs roughly 3–4% of smartphone royalty before the CSS step-up, and the units disappearing are the entry-tier ones with Arm's lowest per-chip royalty, so the royalty-weighted hit is smaller than the unit hit [estimate: IDC states the decline is concentrated in the sub-$200 and sub-$100 tiers, which are Armv8 non-CSS designs]. Note the tension inside management's own numbers: the same call gave "a 2% or 4% at worst impact" and then "4% to 6%" for the same scenario, and by July 2026 the segmentation had been abandoned — "initially the expectation was just going to affect the lower end of the market and now we are seeing all parts of the market, even some upper and mid-tier being affected."

RISC-V in the long tail, Arm competing with its own licensees, the Qualcomm litigation reaching trial in the fourth calendar quarter of 2026, and PRC policy are the other four, covered in Sections 4 and 18.

6.4 What the mix history says about the direction mix can run

The pre-2016 entity is the only place a full cycle is visible, and it shows mix running the other way. From its FY2015 20-F: in CY2014 "US dollar revenues from royalties increased 7% ... The greater increase in royalty units shipped of 16% was due to the mix of products sold by customers ... an increase in demand for ARM-based microcontrollers ... which typically have a lower royalty per chip." The following year royalty grew faster than units on a shift back toward application processors and Armv8-A. Mix is not a ratchet. A single year in which the unit growth arrives in cheap parts turns a 16% volume year into 7% of revenue. Today's ladder runs the favourable way because premium mobile is where the content is concentrated; if the AI-PC, IoT-NPU and low-cost-handset tails supply the next unit wave, the same arithmetic reverses.

7. Critical points

1. Reported growth and arm's-length growth differ by 13.6 points, and the gap is one related party. FY2026 revenue grew 22.8% to $4,920m. Excluding the $704.4m of Consulting Agreement revenue from an unnamed affiliate of SoftBank Group, it grew 9.2%. License and Other revenue grew 25.4% reported and declined 5.4% excluding it. External-customer revenue grew 7.4% while related-party revenue grew 82.1%, and related parties supplied 74% of the year's revenue increase. Of the $704.4m recognised, $645.8m sat in current contract assets at 2026-03-31 — 92% unbilled — falling to $577m at 2026-06-30 as billing began. The stream has no disclosed contract term, no renewal date and no FY2028 run rate, and it plateaued at roughly $200m a quarter three quarters ago. It is 14.3% of FY2026 revenue set by an 86.4% shareholder whose CEO chairs Arm's Remuneration Committee (20-F FY2026, Note 20 and Item 7.B). Section 8 tests management's growth claim against this arithmetic.

2. Royalty growth has been price and mix on a flat unit base since FY2023, and Arm has retired the series that measures it. Royalty grew 56.0% from FY2023 to FY2026 while shipments went from 30.583bn to a bounded 31.3–36.3bn a year. Royalty per chip went from $0.055 to $0.072–$0.084 [estimate]. Per-period chips shipped was disclosed quarterly through the Q4 FY2024 letter, at 7.0bn and down 10% year over year, and has not been published since. The Armv9 share of royalty ran 10% to 15% to 20% to 25% and was withdrawn one quarter after being reaffirmed against a 60–70% target. Annual units and the architecture mix are the two series Arm stopped giving.

3. Both the growth rate and the source of growth changed inside four quarters, and the guidance moved twice. Royalty grew 27% in the December-2025 quarter, 11% in March 2026, 22% in June 2026, and is guided to 13% for September 2026. On 2026-05-06 the CFO said FY2027 royalty growth would be "roughly 20-ish percent for the year ... across each of the quarters ... relatively close to the 20s percent range". On 2026-07-29 he said "probably somewhere closer to the high teens right now", while reaffirming "20% plus" for FY2028 and FY2029 in the same session. The stated cause is memory-driven bill-of-materials inflation, and the segmentation of it widened from the low end of the handset market to "all parts of the market, even some upper and mid-tier". The investor deck published the same day still prints a 20% FYE26–FYE31 royalty CAGR, footnoted "as of March 2026", unrevised. Section 8 sets the deck's royalty-visibility claim against the same evidence.

4. GAAP operating income is now a function of the share price, and in the latest quarter it went backwards on 22% revenue growth. Q1 FY2027 GAAP operating margin was 7.1% against 18.3% for FY2026, and 41.2% non-GAAP in the same quarter. R&D was $838m, 65.0% of revenue, up 29%; share-based compensation was $343m, 26.6% of revenue. The withhold-to-cover method converts part of that into cash, so the cash cost rises with the stock: withholding tax paid on vested shares went $158m (FY2024) to $120m (FY2025) to $529m (FY2026) to $278m in one quarter, and payroll taxes payable rose from $119m to $375m in the June quarter alone. In FY2026 the cash cost of the equity-compensation programme consumed 78% of free cash flow. For a company with no interest expense, that is the functional equivalent of debt service.

5. The chip business resets the margin structure, and management's own plan contains no consolidated margin expansion. At the FY2031 targets — $10bn of IP/CSS at above 65% non-GAAP operating margin plus $15bn of chip at above 30% — blended non-GAAP operating margin is 44.0% against 43.0% actual in FY2026, and blended gross margin is roughly 69% against 97.5% [estimate: management's disclosed revenue split and margin floors combined with its guided chip gross margin; the FY2031 slide discloses operating margins, not gross margins]. The EPS target corroborates it independently: revenue from $4,920m to $25,000m is a 38.4% compound rate and non-GAAP EPS from $1.77 to more than $9 is 38.5%. Earnings are planned to grow at the same rate as revenue, not faster. Revenue rises 5.1x; margin rises one point. Section 8 tests the operating-leverage claim that sits on top of this.

6. Four of the five most consequential drivers in any forward model are things that did not exist as revenue lines eighteen months ago. Moving one driver at a time, FY2031 non-GAAP operating income swings 22.0% on a ±25% change in AGI CPU sockets, 17.5% on a ±20% change in AGI CPU price, 12.9% on the SoftBank Consulting Agreement and 12.3% on AGI CPU gross margin, against 9.6% for Cloud AI royalty growth and 7.3% for smartphone units. The royalty annuity that is 53.1% of today's revenue and the arm's-length licence business that is 26.4% of it together account for less swing than the socket count of a product that has never been sold. Three of the top five are terms in the same equation, none of which Arm has disclosed, and their errors are correlated: a demand shortfall that cuts sockets is the same event that cuts price and delays the scale that lifts gross margin.

7. The forward book has been shrinking for two years while the revenue it supports grew 61%. Remaining performance obligations declined from $2,484m (Mar-2024) to $2,226m to $2,071m, and the share expected within twelve months declined from 28% to 24%. Arm's explanation is "improvements in the timing of revenue conversion", which describes pulling forward, not adding. At 0.42x total revenue and 0.90x licence revenue, Arm's forward book compares with Cadence at 1.47x and Synopsys at 1.62x on the same excluding-future-royalties basis. Some of that gap is recognition policy — Arm books much of its licence fee at a point in time on delivery — and the direction inside Arm is not policy.

8. The advantage is at the architecture, and the returns have not followed it. A 97.5% gross margin converts to an 18.3% GAAP operating margin, a 16.3% GAAP return on invested capital and a 6.4% three-year incremental return. On a non-GAAP basis the same business earns 38.4%. The difference is $1,052m of equity-settled share-based compensation against $900m of GAAP operating income. Whichever basis is used, the direction over three years is down, and the cause is internal reinvestment, not competitive pressure, and it is still what separates this business from the top tier.

9. Arm has become its customers' competitor, and the mechanism that would make that pay is under investigation. The one way the architecture position becomes a chip-level advantage is differential access — the pricing, timing or quality of CPU design licences granted to competing Arm-based CPU vendors — and that is the subject of an FTC monopolisation probe reported in May 2026, a Korea Fair Trade Commission inspection of Arm's Seoul office in November 2025, and a Qualcomm-driven multi-jurisdiction campaign [web]. Arm's FY2026 20-F, filed eleven days after the FTC probe was reported and six months after the Korean inspection, names neither regulator; the entire year-over-year change to its antitrust language is six inserted words ("could and has from time to time" subjected us to investigations). Qualcomm is 9% of FY2026 revenue and its own trial is expected in the fourth calendar quarter of 2026.

10. Capital intensity has tripled before the product it is for has shipped, and it is the one line management does not forecast. Capex went from 2.8% of revenue in FY2024 to 5.5%, to 11.1% in FY2026, to 15.3% annualised in Q1 FY2027, and from 0.57x depreciation to 2.63x. Property and equipment rose from $354m to $923m in fifteen months. Arm guides revenue, non-GAAP operating expense and non-GAAP EPS, and guides neither capex nor free cash flow. On top sit $1,362m of non-cancelable purchase obligations running to 2036 and roughly $100m of semiconductor supply commitments the filing says are "likely to grow materially in subsequent years". There is no inventory line at 2026-06-30 for a product entering production by end-calendar-2026.

8. Management claim pressure tests

"Our third consecutive year, since going public, of more than 20% revenue growth." (Q4 FYE26 shareholder letter and call.) That and its alternative are both accurate statements about the same GAAP revenue. FY2026 revenue grew 22.8%; excluding the SoftBank Consulting Agreement it grew 9.2%, and the licence half declined 5.4%. The Consulting Agreement contributed $559m of a $468m total licence increase, so every other licensing component together declined $91m. The geographic cut says the same thing without touching the related-party note: ex-Japan revenue grew from $3,711m to $4,095m, 10.4%. The most recent quarter partially reverses it — external revenue grew 24.3% against related parties at 18.3%, and external licence revenue grew 21.6% after a full year of decline — so the FY-only characterisation and the quarter-only characterisation are both incomplete. What is not in dispute is that a third of Q1 FY2027 licence revenue and 15.0% of total revenue came from the controlling shareholder's affiliate.

ACV is "maintaining strong momentum" and "above our long-term expectations for license revenue growth." (Q1 FY27 letter.) Annualised contract value grew 28.1%, 27.7%, 27.6%, 21.6% and then 13.4% across five quarters, and sequential additions went $163m, $72m, $20m, $40m, $72m. Both statements are literally consistent with the mid-to-high-single-digit long-term benchmark; together they describe as unchanged a metric that halved in four quarters. The CFO's own decomposition explains the shape: the Q1 FY2026 step-up took ACV growth "from 17-ish percent" on rest-of-world deals "to the kind of the 28% level" because of "this large step-up in the relationship with SoftBank" (Q1 FY26 call). As that lapped, growth reverted toward the arm's-length rate. Whether the SoftBank statements of work are inside ACV is not disclosed and the published definition does not obviously cover a consultancy contract, so the metric offered as the clean measure of licensing cannot be cleaned.

"Remaining performance obligations decreased 7% year-over-year to $2,071 million, impacted by improvements in the timing of revenue conversion." (Q4 FYE26 letter.) A shrinking contracted book described as an operational improvement. Both can be true and they point in opposite directions for durability. RPO has declined for two consecutive years, 17% in total, while the revenue it supports rose 61%, and the near-term conversion share declined from 28% to 24%. The metric moved to annual-only disclosure one quarter after its second decline, though it continues to appear in each 6-K's ASC 606 note.

The Arm AGI CPU has "no material impact to royalty revenue trajectory" and its revenues "are additional to CPU IP and CSS license and royalty revenue." [deck: Arm Everywhere investor session 2026-03-24, slides 43 and 54.] The incrementality argument depends on segmentation — the buyers are those "who simply aren't going to design their own Arm-based CPU" — and that segmentation is coherent. What has not been tested is the boundary. The CEO's own script names SAP moving core database and business application workloads to Arm "starting with AWS Graviton and expanding to the Arm AGI CPU" (Q4 FY26 call), which is additive language about a customer adopting Arm. But Arm's slide 40 is precisely the arithmetic of substitution: a socket sold as an AGI CPU earns roughly $500 of gross profit per $1,000 of price against roughly $100 as a CSS royalty. If 20% of $15bn of FY2031 chip revenue displaced units a licensee would otherwise have shipped, forgone royalty at a roughly 10% CSS rate is $300m, 3.0% of the $10bn IP target; at 50% displacement it is $750m [estimate: management's disclosed CSS royalty rate applied to assumed displacement shares]. Arm has disclosed no test of this, and the retirement of RPO and both licence counts from the quarterly table removes two of the series an outside observer could have used to detect it.

"Every single partner we asked said yes ... about as good an endorsement I could give." (Q4 FY26 call, on more than 50 partners asked to support the silicon strategy.) The 20-F filed three weeks later says customers "may terminate or materially reduce their relationship with us, seek alternative architectures or products, develop their own proprietary architectures, withhold sensitive roadmap information, reduce collaboration on product development, challenge our pricing models or demand more favorable commercial terms, or pursue legal action", and that "the loss of one or more significant licensing customers as a result of these competitive conflicts could have a material adverse effect on our licensing revenue" (Item 3.D). Public endorsement of a launch is not licence-renewal behaviour eighteen months later. What is at stake is 42% of revenue in three customers and 57% in five, with Qualcomm at 9% already litigating. Two named facts sit against the endorsement: Meta, the AGI CPU's lead partner and co-developer, contracted tens of millions of AWS Graviton5 cores in April 2026 and a multi-generational Qualcomm CPU supply agreement for production from the second half of 2028 [web]. The lead-partner framing includes no volume exclusivity.

The neutrality claim. In March 2025 management said "I think we are providing more choice rather than we are competing with our customers" (SoftBank/Arm special call). Through FY2026 the language was "exploring" chiplets and complete SoCs, repeated on three consecutive calls, with the CFO setting three announcement conditions — tape-out, samples back, non-cancelable customer orders. In March 2026 Arm launched production silicon. Those conditions were disclosed in advance and appear to have been honoured. What is missing is any acknowledgment that the earlier position was abandoned. Asked directly in May 2026 how licensees reacted, the CEO answered on ecosystem endorsement, and no royalty base at risk was quantified.

Consensus has modelled the AGI CPU. It has modelled the first billion and not the fifteen. Consensus gross margin runs 97.3% (FY2027), 91.2%, 86.2% and 77.9% (FY2030), and the only disclosed thing that moves Arm's gross margin is the chip. Solving for the hardware revenue implied by that dilution gives $0–40m in FY2027, $859m–$1,041m in FY2028, $2.0–2.5bn in FY2029, $5.3–6.4bn in FY2030 and $11.8–14.3bn in FY2031 [estimate: blended gross margin = 97.3% × (1−h) + chip gross margin × h, solved for the hardware share h at chip gross margins of 40% and 50%; an independent quarterly route reproduces the FY2028 figure within 8%]. So consensus models the $1bn management stands behind for FY2027–FY2028, not the more-than-$2bn of demand disclosed, and by FY2031 has adopted 79–95% of the $15bn target. Near term the street is the conservative party; long term it has taken management's number. The median FY2031 analyst is within 2.2% of management's revenue target and within 10% on EPS, and only 15 to 16 of the 47 covering analysts publish an FY2031 estimate at all.

Operating leverage. [deck: Q1 FYE27 2026-07-29, slide 25, titled "Non-GAAP operating cost growth decelerating".] Consensus non-GAAP operating income and gross margin jointly imply operating expense compounding at 9.9% from FY2027 to FY2031 while revenue compounds at 43.2%, decelerating to +5.1% and +3.3% in the final two years. Against that: non-GAAP operating expense was $733m in Q1 FY2027, up 18% year over year, $734m in Q4 FY2026, up 30%, and Q2 FY2027 is guided to approximately $780m, a 6.4% sequential step in one quarter. R&D intensity is 56.4% of revenue, 21 points above the highest in the reference class, and has risen in three of the last four years. Asked directly whether the chip business's support cost was incremental, the CFO said it "was baked in the numbers that we shared at Arm Everywhere and embedded within our long-term guidance and so already accounted for" — which places the assumption inside management's own model, which consensus has adopted, so the street is not independently testing it.

Arm as an AI earnings-revision name. The revisions have been the smallest in the peer group. Over the trailing year consensus 2026E EPS for Arm was revised −1.0%, against NVDA +50.2%, TSM +60.2%, AVGO +39.9%, AMD +25.1% and MRVL +13.4%. On 2027E Arm is +12.0% against NVDA +95.8%, AVGO +91.2%, TSM +77.7% and AMD +86.9%. Across eleven measurable earnings windows the mean absolute revision to Arm's next-twelve-month consensus EPS was 4.9% against a mean absolute price move of 26.6% over the identical windows. Since listing, forward consensus EPS rose 186% and the price rose 276%; the forward multiple did the rest, ranging from 40x to 184x. Two explanations of the low revisions fit the evidence: that consensus has not yet modelled the AGI CPU, or that it judges a royalty levied at a few dollars a chip cannot scale with the AI dollar the way merchant silicon revenue does. The base-rate work supports the second.

Royalty visibility. "More than 70% of Arm's forecasted royalties are based on rates already under contract" [deck: Q1 FYE27 2026-07-29, slide 21]. That is a statement about rates, not about volumes or revenue, and the contracted percentage declines from 100% (FYE26) to roughly 60% (FYE31). Royalties are expressly excluded from remaining performance obligations, so RPO says nothing about royalty visibility. Weighted by domain, the uncontracted portion is concentrated where the volume is: Cloud AI is roughly 80–90% contracted through FYE31 and Physical AI roughly 95%, while Edge AI — 78% of FYE26 royalty — falls to roughly 45% contracted by FYE31 [deck: Arm Everywhere investor session 2026-03-24, slides 46–49].

The 40x Cloud AI opportunity increase. [deck: Arm Everywhere investor session, slide 39.] The ladder runs $2.4bn of IP/CSS opportunity today, to $24bn of whole-chip value on the same silicon, to $50bn of whole market beyond the largest cloud providers, to more than $100bn by FYE31. Two of those three steps are real expansions of what Arm can sell; the third is market growth. The step that is not addressable is the accelerator block: $245bn of the FYE26 cloud-AI figure and more than $1tn by FYE31 is silicon Arm earns almost nothing on. Excluding it, Arm's own market went from $202.5bn (CY2022) to roughly $290bn (FYE26) and Arm's royalty capture rate on that basis moved from 0.83% to 0.90%, an increase of seven basis points across three years of Armv9 and CSS.

Four key inflections:

  • September 2016, the take-private and the cost of the investment decade. The last clean public year was CY2015: revenue £968.3m ($1,488.6m), operating margin 41.9%, R&D 28.7% of revenue, 3,975 employees. The first audited year on the other side of the five-year hole is FY2021: revenue $2,027m, GAAP operating margin 11.8%, R&D $814m. Across 5.25 years dollar revenue grew about 36%, near 6% a year, while operating margin declined by roughly 30 points and R&D roughly doubled. Both frameworks expense share-based payment and share-based compensation was immaterial in the period, so the margin comparison is not an accounting artefact. Arm bought a decade of product optionality — Neoverse, automotive, GPU, later CSS — and paid for it with the margin structure and five years of growth. The reporting perimeter also changed, since Treasure Data and the IoT platform were carved out, so the comparison is directional and at medium confidence.
  • 2021 to September 2023, refocus and the IPO. The refocus was forced in origin and chosen in execution. The IoT disposals, the Arm China distribution and the Nuvia termination were the clean-up the IPO route required, and each moved an asset to the controlling shareholder. Then the IPO cost showed up: FY2024 revenue rose 21% while GAAP operating income collapsed to $111m on $1,037m of share-based compensation. Anyone anchoring on GAAP operating margin across FY2023–FY2025 is measuring vesting cliffs.
  • FY2024 to FY2026, royalty growth stops being about units. Royalty rose 56% over three years on a unit base that was flat to slightly up. Arm's royalty line is a pricing mechanism described as a volume annuity. It works while there is an Armv8-to-Armv9-to-CSS ladder left to climb and while data-centre content mix keeps rising, and it is far more exposed to a stall in content adoption than to a stall in unit shipments.
  • March 2026, from licensing IP to selling chips, alongside a related-party revenue stream. Both arrived in the same reporting period and both change what "Arm revenue" means. Son's 2016 rationale rested on Arm being structurally non-competitive with its licensees; Arm now sells a data-centre CPU into sockets addressed by Qualcomm, NVIDIA, Amazon and Google, several of which are among its largest customers, while in active litigation with one of them.

Six patterns in how this company behaves:

  • It spends through troughs when it has cover, and the cover is always SoftBank. 2015 R&D step-up, 2016 take-private, five years of roughly 6% revenue growth and a 30-point margin decline. Then FY2024–FY2026: R&D $1,979m to $2,776m, capex $92m to $545m, GAAP operating margin 25.0% to 18.3%. The behaviour repeated.
  • Its unit forecasts miss high and its price-per-unit outcomes surprise up. Son forecast in July 2016 that Arm-based shipments would go from 15bn to 71bn a year in five years; actual FY2021 shipments were 25.3bn, 36% of the forecast. Meanwhile CY2015 royalty grew 31% on 23% unit growth, and FY2023–FY2026 royalty grew 56% on roughly flat units.
  • When a metric stops supporting the growth claims, it gets retired, each time explained as the metric having become less relevant.
  • Contested assets move to the parent. Treasure Data distributed to shareholders, Pelion sold to SoftBank Group Capital, the Arm China stake distributed to Acetone, the Ampere stake cashed out to SoftBank at exactly the carrying value Arm had already marked it down to. Only Artisan went to a third party, and only after Arm was public.
  • It litigates its architecture licence terms and has not yet won.
  • Long-range disclosure is new. Arm gave no multi-year revenue or EPS target between the 2023 IPO and March 2026 and now has a public FYE31 number that will be marked against actuals for five years. Section 14 scores the last time it did this.

The FY2027 policy, subject to a binding vote at the 2026 AGM, does two things. It raises the CEO's PSU maximum from 125% to 200% of target, taking the maximum annual award value from $23.6m to $37.8m on an unchanged $18.9m target, justified as bringing him "in line with the Arm Leadership Team and U.S. market practice" — the 125% cap is the mechanism that has reduced CEO payouts relative to other executives every year (108% against 134% in FY2025; 113% against 153% in FY2026). And it grants a one-time Value Creation Plan award of 425,000 PSUs on market-capitalization milestones: $1.0tn by 2029-03-31 (25%), $1.5tn by 2030-03-31 (cumulative 50%), $2.0tn by 2031-03-31 (cumulative 100%), measured on the rolling average closing price over any 60-day period before the milestone date, vesting two years after each milestone and fully vested by 2033-04-01.

The plan's demanding side is real: a seven-year horizon, a two-year lag between milestone and vest, a continued-employment condition to 2033, clawback, and a hard 100% cap. Three terms of the plan are loose. The metric is market capitalization, not share price or per-share value, so it rises with share issuance — at roughly 1.1bn shares a $2.0tn market capitalization implies $1,818 per ADS against $239.69 at 2026-07-31, and 425,000 PSUs at that price is roughly $0.77bn [estimate: modest dilution from 1,068m shares]. The 60-day window can be satisfied on any qualifying period before the milestone date, not the period immediately preceding it. And the committee may adjust the targets subject only to being "no less challenging as those originally set", a judgment the committee makes. Set against management's own FYE31 targets of $25bn of revenue and more than $9 of EPS, that price is roughly 202x that year's EPS if the ">$9" is read as GAAP; management did not state the basis, and on a non-GAAP basis the implied multiple is lower. Either way the VCP requires delivering the plan and the market continuing to pay a multiple in the same neighbourhood.

Say-on-pay: the free float has been voting against. At the 2025 AGM the remuneration report passed with 953,386,892 for, 15,218,864 against and 1,287,697 withheld — 98.4% headline support. SoftBank held 922,733,999 shares. Subtracting that block, non-SoftBank votes were 30,652,893 for and 15,218,864 against: 33.2% of votes cast by non-SoftBank holders went against [estimate: assumes SoftBank voted its entire block in favour, which the arithmetic requires since the "for" total exceeds SoftBank's holding by only 30.7m votes against a 145.3m-ADS float]. The FY2027 policy requires a binding vote at the 2026 AGM; SoftBank's 86.4% determines it, and both the FY2027 annual award and the VCP were already granted in May 2026 conditional on that approval.

Insider ownership. SoftBank's share count is identical across all three 20-Fs — 922,733,999 — and the percentage decline from 88% to 86.4% is entirely Arm's own issuance for equity compensation. SoftBank has not sold a share, and the CEO's statement that Son "is not interested in selling 1 share of Arm stock" is supported by the filings. The counterweight is that 769,029,000 shares, 72.0% of the company, are pledged under an $8.5bn margin facility whose lenders "may ... exercise their rights to foreclose on and sell or cause the sale of our shares" on an event of default, which "could cause a change of control of us". Candor. Across twelve post-IPO earnings calls, first-person error language — "we were wrong", "mistake", "misjudged", "underestimated", "fell short", "took longer than", "didn't execute" — appears twice, neither an admission: one an analyst's question, one the CFO using "make no mistake" as an intensifier about good news. 11. M&A, divestitures & deal track record

Arm is not a serial acquirer. Across 22 years it did two acquisitions large enough to move the business — Artisan Components in December 2004 for approximately $913m [web] and Treasure Data in August 2018 for a press-reported $600m [web] — and exited both. Everything else was a sub-$100m tuck-in bought for engineers and a narrow IP block; total disclosed consideration for the eleven acquisitions between 2011 and 2015 was roughly $150m. On the two occasions the company bought something that mattered, the outcome was poor, and both episodes happened inside SoftBank private ownership with both exits going to SoftBank affiliates at book value and no independent price discovery. Public shareholders neither funded those purchases nor received the proceeds. That is a real mitigant and it is also the problem: the only part of the record that is gradeable on outcomes is the part public shareholders were not exposed to, and the part they are exposed to is two deals old.

The record attributable to the public company is thin and so far unobjectionable: one disposal of a mature product line to an arm's-length strategic buyer for cash at a $131.0m book gain, and one $265.0m all-cash tuck-in closed 2026-07-01 after slipping past two guided close dates. The absence of impairment proves nothing: Arm has one reporting unit (20-F FY2026, Note 1), so the annual test compares roughly $1.6bn of goodwill to the fair value of the entire company and no individual deal at any outcome could trigger a charge. The exposure is the build decision, not acquisition discipline. FY2026 capex was $545m against $92m two years earlier and R&D rose $705m. Arm is building the AGI CPU, not buying it, and DreamBig at $265.0m is the smaller half of a build-versus-buy answer that has already been given.

11.2 Round-trip arithmetic and the goodwill record

Goodwill has moved only on foreign exchange in every year of the public record: $1,651m (FY2021), $1,636m, $1,620m, $1,625m, $1,620.0m, $1,623.2m (FY2026). No goodwill has ever been written off, in the current record or the legacy one.

Artisan. Paid approximately $913m in December 2004 for physical IP comprising standard cell libraries, embedded memories, I/O cells, analog functions and high-speed interface IP [web]. Sold the foundation-IP subset for $128.5m on 2025-08-26 — 14.1% of the purchase price in nominal dollars after 20.7 years. The two figures are not directly nettable: Arm did not sell everything Artisan brought, and the portfolio generated two decades of licence fees and royalties that Arm reported only inside its consolidated lines. What they establish is that an asset bought as a $913m strategic platform ended as a $128.5m disposal. Management has never mentioned it on an earnings call — not once across all 23 archived earnings-call transcripts and all 11 presentation transcripts.

The gain reconciles to the absence of book value. Cadence paid $128.5m and assumed roughly $2.0m of net liabilities, so Arm's $131.0m gain is almost the whole sale price, because the 2004-vintage intangibles were fully amortized and no goodwill was released. Arm recognized a gain on the sale of a business and derecognized zero goodwill while the buyer recorded $49.7m of goodwill on the same assets. ASC 350-20-40 requires goodwill to be allocated to a disposed business on relative fair values, so either the allocation was judged immaterial or the goodwill was judged not attributable; Arm does not explain the asymmetry, and the effect is that the reported gain understates what Arm gave up.

IoT software. Roughly $600m for Treasure Data [web] plus undisclosed sums for Stream Technologies and Simulity. Exit value $56.2m. Recovery on the disclosed portion: 9.4%, both exits to SoftBank affiliates at carrying value.

Ampere. The implied original cost basis is roughly $75m [estimate: $416.2m carrying value at 2022-03-31 less the $341.2m FY2021 fair-value gain], so the $143.4m exit was roughly 1.9x cost — and a 63% decline from the $389.8m peak carrying value. The FY2025 markdown of $246.4m was taken in the fiscal year ending twelve days after SoftBank's acquisition of Ampere was announced, and Arm then realized exactly the marked-down value from SoftBank. Either the mark anticipated the announced transaction or the transaction cleared at the mark. The filings state both facts and reconcile neither, and no fairness opinion or independent process is disclosed for it or for the 2022 Arm China transfer.

Price renegotiation and slippage. The Artisan sale was announced at "approximately $150.0 million" gross and closed at $128.5m, 14.3% lower, "subject to purchase price adjustments", which was never explained. DreamBig's expected close moved across three successive filings and landed on the first day of the third guided quarter.

12.3 Structural balance-sheet risks, ranked

1. The SoftBank margin loan secured on 72.0% of Arm's equity. SoftBank subsidiaries borrowed $8.5bn in August 2023 secured by 769,029,000 Arm ordinary shares. Arm "has no material obligations with respect to the SoftBank Group Facility" but "entered into certain customary undertakings for the benefit of the lenders to facilitate the pledge ... and we have agreed to facilitate the foreclosure upon the pledge following an event of default". The facility requires prepayment on certain change-of-control events "or in the event the trading price of our ADSs declines below certain thresholds", and can be margin-called if loan-to-value exceeds a threshold. Scale it against the float: the pledged block is 5.3x the entire public float of 145,344,760 ADSs, and a forced sale of even 5% of the pledge would equal 26% of the float. Current coverage is very large — at $239.69 the pledged block is worth roughly $184bn against an original $8.5bn draw, roughly 4.6% loan-to-value on original principal [estimate: the current outstanding balance and the trigger level are not disclosed] — so the risk is structural and severe in shape but not proximate at current prices. The volatility is the caveat: the ADS traded $439.46 on 2026-06-18 and $239.69 six weeks later.

2. Related-party contract-asset concentration. $645.8m of current contract assets owed by an unnamed SoftBank affiliate at 2026-03-31, against $145.5m a year earlier: 66% of total current contract assets, 71% of FY2026 net income and 7.8% of shareholders' equity, backed by a counterparty with no separately assessable credit. The balance declined to $577m at 2026-06-30 while a further $193m was recognised, so billing has begun. Total related-party exposure at 2026-06-30 is $858m.

3. The cash cost of equity compensation, levered to Arm's own share price and inflecting. Covered above. Combined cash outflow of $376m in Q1 FY2027 equalled 42% of operating cash flow and 57% of free cash flow.

4. A step change in capital intensity with no financing plan disclosed. Capex went 1.3% of revenue (FY2022) to 11.1% (FY2026) to 15.3% (Q1 FY2027), and from 0.18x depreciation to 2.63x. Gross property and equipment rose from $800m to $1,324m in FY2026. Against that, Arm has $3,888m of cash, no revolver, no commercial paper programme, no rated debt and no disclosed intention to raise. The base case is self-funding; the point is that no backstop exists if working capital surprises. The early signal is visible: prepayments rose from $169m to $228m, up 35%, in the June quarter.

5. Floating-rate exposure — long, not short. Arm has no floating-rate liabilities and $3,888m of cash and bank deposits generating $111m of net interest income in FY2026, equal to 12.3% of GAAP operating income. A 200bp decline in short rates would cost roughly $70–78m of pre-tax income [estimate: applied to the average cash and short-term investment balance], about 8% of FY2026 GAAP operating income, with no offsetting reduction in borrowing cost.

6. FX mismatch, partially and briefly hedged. Revenue is substantially US dollars; a significant part of the cost base is sterling, with the Cambridge headquarters at 418,793 square feet on leases to 2044, plus euro and renminbi exposure. Hedging is sterling forwards designated as cash-flow hedges, notional £405.0m at 2026-03-31, and "the maximum length of time over which the Company is hedging its exposure ... is 12 months". Total designated notional of $544m is roughly 14% of one year of operating expenses [estimate]. Arm does not quantify the sterling share of operating expense anywhere, which leaves an unmodelled error term on the line the whole forward disagreement turns on.

7. Structural subordination. None inside Arm, because there is no debt. The relevant point runs the other way: Arm China generated $790.6m of FY2026 IPLA revenue and is not consolidated. Arm holds a 10% non-voting interest in Acetone, whose primary asset is 48.2% of Arm China. Arm has no claim on Arm China's assets or cash, and Arm China is simultaneously the largest customer.

8. Covenant proximity. Not applicable. No credit agreement exists and lease agreements contain no material restrictive covenants. There is no credit rating from any agency, because there is no rated debt.

Two prior episodes show what the absence of fixed charges is worth. FY2025 operating cash flow collapsed 64% to $397m on working-capital movements, and free cash flow declined to $99m, with no liquidity consequence. FY2024 GAAP operating income was $111m on $3,233m of revenue because of IPO share-based compensation, again with no balance-sheet consequence. Earnings and cash-flow volatility does not reach solvency here, and that survives the AGI CPU build-out at current commitment levels.

GAAP operating margin was 7.1% in the June-2026 quarter against 18.3% for FY2026, an 11.2-point compression driven entirely by spending: R&D was $838m at 65.0% of revenue against 56.4% for the year, and share-based compensation was $343m at 26.6% of revenue against 21.4%. Non-GAAP operating margin was 41.2%, up 200 basis points year over year, so the whole GAAP deterioration is equity compensation and the AGI CPU engineering build. Any model anchored on the FY2026 GAAP margin overstates the current quarter by more than eleven points. ACV growth decelerated from 28% in each of the first three quarters of FY2026 to 22% and then 13%, while reported licence revenue grew 23% in the same quarter. The committed-fee base is now running ten points behind recognised revenue. And capex reached 15.3% of revenue, against 11.1% for FY2026 and 2.8% two years earlier, while operating cash flow of $902m was 2.7x the year-ago quarter. The working-capital swing runs the other way from the capex.

Reported free cash flow is working-capital-driven in both directions. FY2026 operating cash flow was suppressed by $602m of working-capital movement, chiefly a $309m build in contract assets and a $201m build in receivables, so free cash flow excluding working capital was $1,484m against the reported $882m. Q1 FY2027 was flattered by $389m in the other direction, chiefly a favourable receivables swing and an increase in other liabilities the filing attributes to "timing of payroll taxes and higher outstanding balance of employment taxes payable on vested shares" — a bill payable in a later quarter — so free cash flow excluding working capital was roughly $276m against the reported $665m. The CFO's "$665 million for the quarter and $1.4 billion over the trailing 12 months" is accurate as reported and overstates the repeatable rate.

13.4 What drove the numbers

Revenue. Foreign exchange is not a factor: "Less than 2% of our total revenue is denominated in currencies other than U.S. dollars, and the impact of changes in foreign exchange rates on our revenue for the fiscal years ended March 31, 2026 and 2025 was immaterial" (20-F FY2026, Item 5.A). Arm completed no acquisitions in FY2022–FY2026 and one divestiture, and the Artisan disposal was not treated as discontinued operations, so FY2026 contains roughly five months of Artisan revenue against twelve in FY2025 and organic growth is understated by an amount Arm does not break out. The FY2026 revenue increase of $913m decomposes as related-party licence and other +$591m (64.7%), external royalty +$360m (39.4%), related-party royalty +$85m (9.3%) and external licence and other −$123m (−13.5%). Of the $676m related-party increase, $558.9m is the single SoftBank Consulting Agreement.

Gross margin. Cost of sales declined in absolute terms from $145m (FY2021) to $121m (FY2026) while revenue grew 2.4x, because it is services delivery and support. The FY2026 20-F attributes the flat figure to "decreases in activities associated with professional and design services, offset by increases in salaries and related expenses, share-based compensation costs and depreciation" — professional and design services activity declined inside cost of sales in the year design-services revenue rose $559m, which is either evidence the SoftBank cost is booked in R&D or evidence the arm's-length design-services business shrank sharply. Neither explanation is disclosed.

Operating margin, in basis points of revenue. FY2023 to FY2024, −2,160bp, of which gross margin −80, R&D −1,890 and SG&A −200: share-based compensation went from $79m to $1,037m, a $958m increase against a combined R&D-plus-SG&A increase of $1,067m, so equity compensation explains about 90% of it, concentrated in the September-2023 IPO quarter which recorded $518m of it. FY2024 to FY2025, +1,730bp, the reversal. FY2025 to FY2026, −250bp, and this one is different in kind: share-based compensation rose $232m but R&D rose $705m, with the balance cash — headcount up 15% to 9,584, engineers up 16% to 8,058, plus cloud services, IT, engineering expense, allocated facility overhead and $66m more depreciation and amortization. Arm's own non-GAAP operating margin, which strips equity compensation entirely, still declined from 46.7% to 43.0%, so the FY2026 compression is real cash spending.

Returns. Invested capital went from $1,836m (FY2023) to $4,685m (FY2026). Roughly a third of the $2,849m increment is contract assets — revenue recognised but not yet billable — of which $645.8m is the SoftBank affiliate alone. About a fifth is the property and equipment build. Three-year incremental return on invested capital was 6.4%.

Cash conversion swings sharply, and the instability is working capital, not earnings. Free cash flow ÷ net income ran 2.65x, 0.63x, 1.16x, 2.96x, 0.13x, 0.98x across the six years. FY2021 included a $950m inflow from contract liabilities that is not repeatable and flatters any long-run average built from it. FY2025's $99m on $792m of net income reflects $412m absorbed by contract assets, $331m by receivables, $381m by other liabilities and $152m by accrued compensation. Cumulative free cash flow of $3,870m against cumulative net income of $3,463m over six years is a 1.12x conversion, and only one of the six years is near the average.

Working capital deserves a second look because the headline improves while the underlying worsens. Receivables-only days sales outstanding went 151.8 (FY2022), 136.1, 88.2, 100.8, 96.4 (FY2026), a 55-day improvement that looks like a working-capital success. Including contract assets the same measure went 187.7, 172.9, 153.3, 190.8, 192.6, so the collection lag is longer today than in FY2023. At the same time the customer-funded side halved as a share of revenue: contract liabilities were 41.7% of revenue in FY2022 and 21.3% in FY2026, and 18.2% on annualised Q1 FY2027 revenue. Arm is financing progressively more of its own revenue. Days of inventory, days payable and the cash conversion cycle cannot be computed: Arm has held no inventory at any balance-sheet date and does not present accounts payable as a separate caption.

Definition changes that break comparability, in order of importance: the September 2023 change in reporting entity, under which Arm Limited's historical financials became Arm Holdings plc's, retrospectively adjusted; the FY2021–FY2022 presentation of the IoT Platform and Treasure Data as discontinued operations, which makes net income for those two years lower than continuing-operations net income; the Artisan disposal not treated as discontinued operations, so prior periods were not restated; the FY2024 recast of the non-GAAP operating expense definition to exclude employer taxes on equity-classified awards, "recast across historical periods for trend purposes", which means the non-GAAP series is consistently defined only from FY2024; and the FY2026 reclassification of finance-lease right-of-use assets out of property and equipment, restated for FY2025 only, which puts a small break in the PP&E series.

17. Forward model summary

The model is built in three pieces because the consolidated income statement cannot be projected as one thing. From FY2028 the company contains a 97%-gross-margin licensing business and a 40%-gross-margin hardware business, and by FY2031 the hardware business is more than half of revenue in the base case. Modelling one blended margin line would be wrong from the first shipping quarter. Royalty is built as units times royalty per unit across four end markets; licence is built by counterparty, with the SoftBank Consulting Agreement modelled as its own driver because it has no external demand driver of any kind; and the chip line is built as sockets times price. It is an operating model that stops at free cash flow. No valuation, no multiples, no discounting.

The base case implies second-half revenue of $3,191m, +16.8% on the prior second half; consensus implies $3,407m, +24.7%; and the guided Q2 growth rate is +21.6%. That is the arithmetic consequence of growing chip revenue 11x in four years while holding engineering headcount growth at 6–8%, and it should be treated as a constraint on the bull case.

Three things are new to Arm and appear in no consensus line. Inventory: Arm has never held any and there is none at 2026-06-30, and the base case builds $976m of it by FY2031 at 75 days of chip cost of sales. Capital intensity: capex went 2.4% of revenue in FY2023 to 15.3% annualised in Q1 FY2027 before a chip shipped, and the base case holds the dollar run-rate while letting the ratio fall to 7.7%, which still puts FY2031 capex at $1,454m against a consensus $862m. Withholding tax on vested awards is a financing outflow that scales with the share price, and in FY2026 it consumed 60% of free cash flow. For a company with no interest expense it is the functional equivalent of debt service.

No scenario applies a haircut to revenue or margin. Each is a set of driver changes.

Bear — six deltas. Smartphone handset units at −2.7%/yr against −0.2% (Gartner models −8.4% for CY2026 against IDC's −0.9%, and the CFO has widened the stated cause from the low end to all tiers). Royalty per handset at +7.2%/yr against +11.2%. Cloud AI royalty at +25.0%/yr against +41.7% (hyperscalers respond to Arm competing with them by accelerating captive designs and renegotiating architecture terms, a cost that shows up in licence terms). The SoftBank Consulting Agreement running off to zero by FY2031 against $800m flat. AGI CPU sockets at 430 thousand in FY2031 against 950 thousand, roughly $2.9bn of chip revenue. And engineering headcount growing 12%/yr for two years before being forced to roughly 1%, because Arm has over-invested into a slowdown once already.

18. Risks

1. Related-party revenue supplies reported growth, and the arm's-length business grew 7%. Two related parties supplied 30.5% of FY2026 revenue. Arm China is the largest customer at 16%, recognised net, with Arm dependent on Arm China for the financial information used to compute Arm's own revenue. The SoftBank affiliate supplied 14.3%, up from 3.6%, with 92% of it unbilled at year end. External revenue grew 7.4% against related parties at 82.1%. Leading indicators: the quarterly SoftBank contribution management now volunteers, which has plateaued at $193–200m for three quarters; related-party contract assets, which declined $69m in the June quarter as billing began; and the allowance for expected credit losses, which went $3m → $20m → $31m across three years. Mitigation: audit-committee review of related-party transactions above $1.5m with board escalation above $20m, and a critical audit matter naming "an evaluation of whether the transaction price and corresponding performance obligations were fairly presented for related party transactions". The Shareholder Governance Agreement exempts arrangements existing at IPO.

2. Channel conflict with licensees, now an active antitrust matter. Arm sells finished CPUs into the same hyperscale accounts whose licence fees and royalties are the business, and the dispute is already before regulators, originating with a customer that is 9% of revenue. Chronology: Arm sued Qualcomm and Nuvia in August 2022; Qualcomm counter-sued in April 2024; Arm served a 60-day notice of breach asserting a right to terminate Qualcomm's own architecture licence in October 2024 and withdrew it in January 2025; the jury found for Qualcomm in December 2024; Qualcomm filed antitrust complaints across multiple jurisdictions in March 2025 and added a claim in June 2025 that Arm breached the Technology Licence Agreement "by failing to provide license offers at commercially reasonable prices and terms"; final judgment for Qualcomm on 2025-09-30, now on appeal; the Korea Fair Trade Commission inspected Arm's Seoul office on 2025-11-19; Arm launched the AGI CPU in March 2026; and the FTC opened a probe reported on 2026-05-15 [web]. Trial on Qualcomm's claims is expected in the fourth calendar quarter of 2026. Exposure: an adverse remedy constraining architecture-licence pricing or terms applies across the whole licence book, not one account. Leading indicators: ACV growth, already broken from +28% to +13%; any disclosure of a civil investigative demand; the trial outcome. The one indicator that would have shown new-licence formation — the Total Access and Flexible Access counts — is now dark.

3. Royalty deceleration from smartphone bill-of-materials inflation. Mobile applications processors are 43% of royalty, or $1,124m. Cutting FY2027 royalty growth from 20% to 17.5% costs roughly $65m of revenue [estimate]; the CFO's own February worst case of 6% within smartphones on a 20% unit decline is roughly $67m [estimate]. Both land near 1.3% of FY2026 revenue. The dollar risk is small; the credibility risk is not, because the estimate moved from "1%, maybe 2% negative impact on total royalties" confined to the low end (February 2026) to a full-year cut and a +13% quarterly guide (July 2026) inside five months. Realized royalty growth swings 11 points quarter to quarter on a metric that is an accrual estimate trued up in arrears, so it cannot be underwritten off a single full-year figure. Leading indicators: MediaTek and Qualcomm handset unit guidance, DRAM contract pricing, and Arm's own quarterly royalty print against the guide.

4. Production silicon brings an operating model Arm has never run. Wafers, substrates, test capacity and memory bought and held; warranty and performance-guarantee obligations; first-generation gross margin of 38–42% against 97.2%. The FY2026 20-F added an entire cluster of new risk factors for this: demand forecasting for third-party-manufactured products, roughly $100m of purchase commitments expected to "grow materially", "margin compression, greater quarter-to-quarter revenue volatility, increased capital intensity and working capital requirements", product liability and warranty exposure with the concession that "we may not have adequate insurance coverage or reserves to cover all potential claims", and multi-generation execution risk naming node transitions, lead-partner coordination and inventory across generations. Leading indicators, in order of usefulness: the first inventory line on the balance sheet, which does not exist at 2026-06-30; whether chip revenue is recognised gross or net under the new third-party-arrangement policy, because a gross conclusion on a large pass-through would inflate revenue at near-zero margin; the purchase-obligation footnote in the FY2027 20-F; and the first quarter consolidated gross margin moves off 97%. Mitigation: the compute die is the CSS, already funded inside IP R&D, so the incremental development cost is lower than for a standalone chip company, and the balance sheet absorbs mistakes.

5. SoftBank control, the pledged-share overhang and the CEO's split role. SoftBank owns 86.4% and 72.0% of the company is pledged under an $8.5bn margin facility — 83.3% of SoftBank's own stake [estimate] — with prepayment triggers tied to the ADS price and a foreclosure mechanism that "could cause a change of control of us". The CEO said in February 2026 that Son "is not interested in selling 1 share of Arm stock"; the 20-F filed three months later describes a mechanism under which shares can be sold without SoftBank choosing to. Separately, the CEO became part-time CEO of SoftBank Group International in April 2026, and the 20-F acknowledges "competing demands for Mr. Haas's time and attention" — the same executive now sits on both sides of the agreement supplying 14.3% of revenue. Leading indicators: SoftBank Group's disclosed loan-to-value and net debt, amendments to the facility, and the ownership percentage in each successive 20-F.

6. Arm China: unconsolidated, net-recognised, and the largest customer. Arm holds a 10% non-voting interest in Acetone, whose primary asset is 48.2% of Arm China; Arm has no board seat of its own. Revenue is a percentage of what Arm China earns from its own PRC sublicences and is recognised net, so Arm's PRC revenue is not PRC market revenue and a change in the undisclosed revenue-share percentage would look exactly like a change in PRC demand. IPLA revenue was $670.8m, $670.4m then $790.6m; the net amount due from Arm China at 2026-03-31 was $276.2m. The most recent quarter is the first year-over-year decline in the disclosed series, −5.8%, with the licence share down 24%. The FY2026 20-F rewrote the PRC-policy paragraph around "local requirements" and a "government-wide" self-sufficiency push.

7. Qualcomm litigation. Qualcomm is 9% of FY2026 revenue, roughly $443m [estimate]. How these correlate: the bad-quarter scenario. They do not arrive independently, and the second half of FY2027 is where four converge. Memory prices raise handset bills of materials (royalty units down), raise AGI CPU cost of goods (gross margin below the guided band) and tighten AGI CPU supply (revenue below the $1bn), so one input produces three hits. The SoftBank licence stream laps, so reported licence growth cannot lean on the same comparison from Q1 FY2028. The Qualcomm trial lands one quarter before first AGI CPU revenue, with the FTC and Korean matters unresolved behind it. And management has flagged FY2027 as a year of long-term renewals with "some of the world's largest hyperscalers, automotive and robotics companies and handset OEMs" — renewals negotiated while the licensor is a competitor and under antitrust investigation. The compounding version is royalty growth in the low teens, licence growth falling to mid single digits as the related-party stream laps and external licensing stays soft, and AGI CPU revenue arriving at a gross margin nearer 35% than 40%, producing a quarter with decelerating revenue and the first visible dilution of consolidated gross margin. With $3.9bn of cash and no debt, that is an earnings-trajectory event, not a solvency one.

The peer precedent for the channel-conflict mechanism. Imagination Technologies, April 2017: Apple, which analysts put at 50–60% of group sales, said it would no longer use Imagination's IP "in its new products in fifteen months to two years' time". The stock declined roughly 70% in a single session and the company was subsequently sold to Canyon Bridge (City A.M., accessed 2026-08-03) [web]. The mechanism — an architecture licensee replacing the licensor in-house — is exactly the one Arm's largest customers are best placed to execute, since Apple, Qualcomm, NVIDIA, Amazon, Google and Microsoft all already design their own cores under Arm architecture licences. The counterweight is that Apple's replacement GPU was not an instruction-set substitution, whereas leaving Arm means leaving the software ecosystem; RISC-V is the path that removes that counterweight.

20. What we still don't know / monitoring KPIs

Ten unresolved gaps, in the order in which answering them would change a conclusion above.

  1. FY2026 and FY2027 royalty unit volumes. The per-period chips-shipped KPI was retired after Q4 FY2024 and the cumulative counters are rounded floors that cannot be differenced. Royalty per chip for the two most recent years is an estimate with a $0.072–$0.084 range, so the units-versus-rate decomposition that Sections 1 and 6 both depend on cannot be closed for the most recent year. This is the single monitoring item that would most improve the model. It would be resolved by one line in a shareholder letter.
  2. The architecture-licence royalty rate relative to a CSS rate. The claim that architecture licensees pay less appears in four places in the underlying work and is quantified in none; the disclosed per-core ladder — roughly 1.0x, 1.5x, 2.0x, 3.0x across Armv9 and CSS generations — has no architecture-licence rung at all. The largest architecture licensees are exactly the customers driving Cloud AI growth, so the mix effect inside the fastest-growing driver is signed but not sized.
  3. The identity of the 12% third-largest FY2026 customer. Arm China is 16% and the SoftBank affiliate is near-certainly 14%. Whether the third is Apple, MediaTek, Samsung or NVIDIA decides whether the remaining 58% is a diversified book or one in which a single commercial customer is roughly a fifth of arm's-length revenue.
  4. The reconciliation of Cloud AI royalty growth against US-headquartered revenue growth. US revenue grew 2.6% in FY2026 and 1.6% in the June quarter while total revenue grew 22.8% and 22.4%, and every named Cloud AI customer is US-headquartered. This is flagged at the top of Section 6 because it bears directly on that section's conclusion.
  5. FY2017–FY2020 financials and headcount. Reachable from UK Companies House statutory accounts and from SoftBank Group's own segment disclosure, neither attempted. The plan-versus-delivered comparison in Section 14 uses audited endpoints with an unobservable path, and the model's engineering-headcount driver has no pre-IPO base.
  6. The sustainable non-GAAP tax rate and its sensitivity to royalty geography. No filing or call explains the swing from a $94m benefit (FY2024) and $72m benefit (FY2025) to a $253m expense (FY2026). The FY2026 rate reconciliation is dominated by foreign withholding taxes — PRC 7.0 points, Taiwan 4.4, Korea 2.6, Singapore 1.6, 15.6 points of adders in total — which scale with exactly the Asian royalty mix the growth case requires, while management's ">$9" target and this model's $6.01 both apply a roughly 14% rate.
  7. GPU, NPU and System IP: royalty contribution and attach trend. No attach rate, revenue or share is disclosed anywhere. If Arm's GPU attach is eroding — Qualcomm ships Adreno, Apple ships its own GPU, Imagination is still shipping — that is a per-chip rate headwind running against the Armv9 and CSS tailwind, and the exposure can be named but not sized.
  8. The sterling share of operating expense and its hedge coverage. Mentioned once, in a risk list, with no quantification. Operating expense is the second-largest sensitivity in the model and the entire quantified disagreement with consensus is about its growth rate, so a 10% currency move is an unmodelled error term on the one line the argument turns on.
  9. Arm China's IPLA revenue-share percentage and the gross PRC licence pool behind the net $790.6m. Without it, Arm's PRC revenue cannot be tied to any observable PRC market quantity, and a change in the percentage would look exactly like a change in PRC demand.
  10. Any quantified foundry, substrate, test or memory purchase commitment for the AGI CPU. None is disclosed anywhere as of the Q1 FY2027 6-K, against a CEO statement that capacity for the $1bn is secured.

A request queue backing up with GPUs idle in a waiting state at 0% utilisation, behind the claim that a gigawatt AI data centre needs about 30 million CPU cores today and about 120 million once agentic workloads dominate [deck: Arm Everywhere keynote, slides 22–31]. Appendix C — External data sources

Two external datasets were identified for Arm and neither was pulled. This is a menu, not fetched data: no figure above comes from either.

1. memory_spot_pricing — DRAM and NAND spot and contract pricing. Status: not pulled. Access is a web mosaic assembled from scattered cited points, because TrendForce and the other primary vendors are paid; a pull would therefore come with a "partial mosaic, not comprehensive" caveat. What it measures: the trend in memory spot and contract prices, by product and by month.

Why it matters here more than for most companies. Memory reaches Arm through two independent channels and both matter. First, memory-driven bill-of-materials inflation is the CFO's stated reason for cutting the multi-year royalty growth expectation from around 20% to the high teens — the single largest downgrade in the record and the pivot of the model's bear case — and mobile applications processors are 43% of FY2026 royalty. Second, memory is one of four inputs the CEO named as tight for the Arm AGI CPU ramp, and it is also the most plausible explanation for the unreconciled 47–56% of chip revenue in the guided gross margin (§1.4), where bundling 384 GB of DDR5 per socket blends a 90%-margin chip to 47.9% and 768 GB blends it to 33.7%. A pulled series would test the CFO's attribution independently and would date the inflection in handset bills of materials against Arm's own royalty prints, strengthening §6 (secular trends) and the operational-KPI discussion in §13.5. The scattered memory-price points used above are individually cited web references, not a series.

2. sec_xbrl_concept — SEC XBRL companyconcept, peer financials. Status: not pulled. Access is the free SEC XBRL companyconcept API with no key required. What it measures: a named annual financial concept pulled on a consistent tag basis for Arm and each named peer, giving like-for-like peer benchmarking without the hand-reconciliation that the peer tables in §3.1 and §4.3 currently require.

The full peer set — QCOM, NVDA, AVGO, INTC, AMD, MRVL, CDNS and SNPS — all file with SEC XBRL, so the pull would be complete. Two caveats apply. Arm files a 20-F but tags in us-gaap, so it is retrievable on the same basis as the domestic filers. And Arm's fiscal year ends 31 March while most of the peer set is calendar or near-calendar, so the fiscal-year labels are offset by roughly one quarter — the same offset that already requires the most-recent-quarter columns in §3.1 to provide the apples-to-apples comparison. A pull would firm up the returns-quality table in §3.1, where two peer ROIC figures are currently coarse estimates and several are not computed at all, and would let the peer capital-return comparison in §10.3 be completed for QCOM, INTC, AMD, CDNS and SNPS, which were left out of that table for budget reasons.
