Claims by section
business-model
  • That is price taken under scarcity and partly to recover cost, not price taken from a product advantage, and the distinction matters for whether it persists.
  • The binding constraint on Intel's revenue is now a process node that entered high-volume manufacturing in 2017, and Intel is adding wafer starts to it in 2026.
  • Intel's cost of sales as a percentage of revenue went from 44.6% in FY2021 to 65.2% in FY2025 while depreciation went from 12.6% of revenue to 20.4% — depreciation alone explains roughly 38% of the gross-margin collapse since FY2021.
  • The new parts are multi-tile, Foveros-packaged, and partly built by TSMC, which is structurally more expensive than the fully-depreciated monolithic Intel 7 part they replace.
  • But the 10-Q discloses that operating expense was $249M favorable, so the change in cost of sales was +$192M against a change in revenue of +$98M: CCG's gross profit declined by roughly $94M in the quarter Intel raised client prices 16%.
  • A dollar of volume in DCAI is worth almost nothing; a dollar of price is worth about 65 cents.
  • It grew more than 50% in 2025, 26% sequentially in Q4 2025, reached an annualized run-rate above $1bn, and in Q1 2026 grew more than 30% sequentially and nearly doubled year over year.
  • Intel Foundry is not yet a foundry business. It is Intel's manufacturing cost centre with a revenue line attached.
  • Intel spends 3.2x what TSMC spends to produce a dollar of market-valued silicon.
  • Intel Foundry needs roughly **$30–36bn of revenue** to break even at the operating line, 1.7x to 2.0x FY2025
capitalization
  • Partner cash comes in as non-controlling interest and capital in excess of par value, so it increases equity and **reduces reported net debt**, while the partner's economic claim behaves like senior, expensive capital against a specific fab.
competitive-advantage
  • Intel no longer has a durable competitive advantage in any of its three businesses. What it has is a large, decaying incumbency in x86, an unproven option in advanced packaging, and a manufacturing asset base that has been a cost, not a moat for five years running.
  • The moat Intel actually had was never the x86 instruction set. It was process leadership — being one node ahead, so that an Intel die at the same performance was smaller and cheaper than anyone else's.
  • AMD's EPYC and Ryzen are binary-compatible x86. Moving a workload from Xeon to EPYC recompiles nothing and re-qualifies no software.
  • TSMC spread $40,900M of FY2025 capex across the whole fabless world and earned a 50.8% operating margin. Intel spread $14,646M across its own volume alone and earned roughly zero. Same capital intensity, a fifth of the volume base to amortize it over.
  • And TSMC's Q1 2026 operating margin of **58.1% now exceeds Intel's gross margin of 39.4%**.
  • A moat being competed away shows a *narrowing* premium. Intel has no premium left to narrow.
  • Intel's client business is 2.4x AMD's by revenue and earns roughly half AMD's margin per dollar once the fab is charged to it.
  • AMD's Data Center segment earned **$3,603M** of FY2025 operating income against DCAI's $3,422M on **$284M less revenue**, and in the March-2026 quarter AMD's Data Center segment at $5,775M was **larger than all of DCAI** at $5,052M, on the same fiscal close date
  • And the structure works against it: the 10-K concedes "many of the largest potential foundry customers are fabless semiconductor companies whose products compete with our own," so Intel's vertical integration is a customer-acquisition liability.
competitive-landscape
  • a legally-constructed duopoly at the x86 layer inside a slightly wider oligopoly at the PC layer. The barrier that keeps ARM out does not keep AMD out.
  • Flat client revenue against a −11% unit TAM is an implicit forecast of roughly +11–12% client ASP for the year, and it depends on scarcity persisting.
  • The pool grew $6,389M from FY2023 to FY2025. **AMD captured $5,989M of it — 93.7%.** Intel captured $400M, 6.3% of the growth in a market it entered holding 85%.
  • The counter-consensus fact in this segment: AMD now realizes a higher client CPU ASP than Intel.
  • Intel's share declined by two-thirds in two years without losing revenue — DCAI grew 5.9% over the same period. The market grew 3.4x around it.
  • The market Intel leads is worth about one-eighth of the market it does not meaningfully serve.
  • scaling Mercury's x86 shares by the non-ARM share of total shipments gives Intel 67.8% of *all* server CPU units in Q1 2025 and **58.0%** in Q1 2026 — a 9.8-point loss in one year
  • This is a monopoly with a fringe, not an oligopoly.
  • TSMC gets 5.4x more revenue from each dollar of fab asset. Intel spent $89.2bn of capex across 2022–2025 and produced $1.0bn of cumulative external foundry revenue across 2023–2025: about 1.1% of the capex.
  • Intel Foundry's external revenue has shrunk, not inflected.
critical-points
  • If the transfer price does equal market, Intel's cost of manufacture is roughly 58% above market and CCG's margin exists only because another segment absorbs the gap.
  • Q1 2026 is the cleanest data point and the most damaging: revenue grew 16.2% year over year and the operating loss **widened**, from $2,320M to $2,437M.
  • The 14A gate resolves in the second half of 2026, and it is the only truly binary thing in the file.
expectations
  • The same team beats its 90-day guide 73–77% of the time and has missed every multi-year target it has ever set.
  • The base rate of Intel hitting its own multi-year model is 0 for 11 lines.
  • no IDM has ever built a leading-edge merchant foundry of scale.
financial-history
  • Gross margin declined 26.2 points from 61.0% to 34.8% and declined in **every one of the seven years from FY2018 to FY2024**, with no year of respite — while revenue kept *rising* for three of those years.
  • DCAI's operating margin ran 6.4 / 9.2 / 8.6 in the last three quarters of FY2024, then 13.9 / 16.1 / 23.4 / 26.4 / 30.5 across the next five.
forward-model
  • Consensus is not the base case; consensus is the bull case.
  • All three scenarios have Intel losing share for seven straight years.
  • Consensus asks Intel to run a cash gross margin at or above its all-time best, with double the depreciation burden and no process leadership.
  • The consensus gap is not a foundry gap. It is a revenue-and-product-margin gap that a foundry turnaround alone cannot close.
secular-trends
  • That $74bn gap is the secular story in one number, and it is not cyclical: Intel's revenue peaked in FY2021 and has declined 33.1% since, through a period in which the industry grew every year.
  • This is not a portfolio-pruning story with a healthy core underneath. Both cores shrank by about the same amount.
  • The AI PC is a mix lever, not a volume lever.
  • This is the central secular fact about Intel and it cannot be undone by a good server year.
  • Being levered to a trend you cannot sell into is a cost, not an asset, until a named 14A customer appears.
technology
  • The technology where Intel is differentiated, has external design interest, and prices at a premium is **advanced packaging**, a 2017-vintage capability that does not require Intel to win the node race at all.
  • TSMC does not ship backside power on N2; it arrives with A16. On that one axis Intel is ahead of TSMC by about a generation.
  • Density sets cost per transistor, which is the first question a fabless foundry customer asks; performance sets price. Intel has traded toward performance, which suits its own high-ASP CPUs and suits a merchant foundry less well.
  • The CFO says foundry-average gross margin is "multiple, multiple quarters" away.
  • Of seven dated technology commitments Intel has made in the last six years — 7nm on schedule, five nodes in four years, Intel 20A, process parity in 2024 and leadership in 2025, 18A manufacturing readiness in 2H 2024, the first 18A SKU by end-2025, and the Ohio fab — **one was met on the stated terms**, and it was the smallest (a single SKU by a quarter-end).
  • After five years and more than $100bn of net PP&E, the leading-edge wafer business has no significant external customer by the company's own admission, while the packaging business nobody models has demand management sizes in the billions per year.
value-chain
  • Intel is in the middle of a chain in which **every adjacent layer earns a better return than it does**.
  • The OEM out-earns the chip designer in absolute operating dollars per PC, and once Intel is charged for its own fabs its economic take on that PC is negative.
  • Intel's fabs cap how many units it can sell; its suppliers cap how much it can earn on each one.
  • This is the ceiling on Intel's new pricing power, and it is close.
  • Net effect: Intel's newly-won power over its customers is worth less than it looks, because it is passed straight through to memory makers, ASML and substrate suppliers.

That is price taken under scarcity and partly to recover cost, not price taken from a product advantage, and the distinction matters for whether it persists.
